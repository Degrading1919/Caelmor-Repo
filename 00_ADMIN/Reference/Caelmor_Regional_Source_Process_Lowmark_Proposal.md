# Caelmor Regional Source / Process Foundation — Lowmark Vale

**Status:** proposal-stage connective lore for Creative Director review. This brief establishes enough source, labor, process, risk, stewardship, scarcity, and cultural context for later material derivation. It does not approve resources, items, recipes, nodes, tiers, schemas, exact routes, or production volumes, and it does not amend live canon.

## Authority and limits

This proposal uses the reconciled Lowmark dossier, Timeline, War History, Factions, Parts 2–6, and the canon-reconciliation audit. Legacy named resources and finished goods show earlier functional intent only.

- The C09 layered model controls all water-dependent work: simple farming water practice is openly taught by households and neighbors; experienced community stewards oversee shared works; formal charter oversight applies only to larger irrigation; combat water magic belongs only to the Last Field-Mage.
- Ordinary washing, soaking, cooling, clay preparation, and transport do not become magical processes merely because they use water.
- No proposal resolves C03, C06, or C07, fixes exact office boundaries, defines a route, or creates a player spell system.
- Lowmark remains the only full v1 region.

## Regional proposition

Lowmark's material foundation grows from maintained water, productive soil, crossings, household labor, and constant repair. Its strengths are access and transformation at modest scale rather than an exceptional mineral or industrial identity. Material authority should follow demonstrated duties: keeping a bank stable, a crossing usable, a shared channel clean, a kiln from smoking a neighbor's home, or a work yard from fouling water.

## Foundation 1 — forestry and timber stewardship

| Required field | Proposed foundation |
|---|---|
| World source or import dependency | Managed riparian and communal working woodland can supply ordinary repair wood, while reeds, orchards, old structures, and imported structural stock supplement it. The proposal does not select species, grades, woodland sites, or exclusive sources. |
| Who gathers or imports it | Local cutters, river households, orchard workers, carriers, and market traders move stock; bridge, mill, ferry, dike, building, and boat crews create demand. Thornfell may supply some structural or specialized stock through broad exchange. |
| Who processes it | Yard workers, sawyers in the broad occupational sense, carpenters, boat and bridge repairers, handle makers, household workers, and fletching workers season, shape, join, and repair wood. No named guild is created. |
| Broad processing method | Selective cutting, removal from vulnerable banks, sorting, seasoning, straightening, shaping, joining, patching, and safe reclamation are sufficient. War-scorched or waterlogged timber requires inspection before reuse. |
| Fuel and water needs | River movement can assist handling; seasoning needs protected time and air. Woodworking uses modest fuel, while drying and glue-like binding work compete with household heat without defining a recipe. |
| Waste and risk | Poor cutting can erode banks, expose channels, damage orchards, or remove future repair stock. Fire, saw waste, rot, pests, unstable salvaged beams, and blocked waterways are ordinary hazards. |
| Stewardship and access | Councils, landholders, river households, and crossing or repair keepers can delay cutting where bank stability, public repair, or seasonal need outweighs immediate sale. Exact legal jurisdiction remains open. |
| Scarcity reason | Flood damage, war loss, seasoning time, bank protection, competing household and civic repair, and limited access to sound mature stock create scarcity. |
| Regional cultural meaning | Sound timber represents care extended forward: a tree left standing may protect a bank or future bridge. Repaired beams and boats carry household history without becoming magical evidence. |
| Broad cross-region dependency | Thornfell has mountain wood and engineering precedent; Emberholt demands imported timber. Lowmark can participate in both relationships without a fixed route, quota, or export monopoly. |

## Foundation 2 — metal and stone sourcing

| Required field | Proposed foundation |
|---|---|
| World source or import dependency | Lowmark can support limited ordinary stone gathering or controlled pit work where later map review allows it, but it does not have an approved complete metal source. Most conventional metal may arrive as ore, stock, tools, or repairable pieces through markets. Legacy named mines and metals are not promoted. |
| Who gathers or imports it | Pit or bank crews, stone dressers, carriers, market traders, bridge workers, and charter or council agents hold different parts of the chain. Thornfell and Emberholt provide broad metal and stone precedents, not guaranteed suppliers. |
| Who processes it | Stone dressers and builders prepare ordinary stone; repair smiths and tool keepers assess, mend, and reuse conventional metal. Any primary smelting share remains open rather than assumed. |
| Broad processing method | Controlled extraction, sorting, haulage, dressing, fitting, repair, and finite reclamation provide the foundation. Battlefield and memorial material require custody review before any reuse. |
| Fuel and water needs | Stone work needs haulage and dust control; metal repair needs accounted fuel and clean cooling water. Shared-water practice governs access socially but does not enhance the material magically. |
| Waste and risk | Unstable faces, bank collapse, dust, spoil, smoke, hot scale, sharp scrap, contaminated cooling water, and false salvage claims burden workers and neighbors. |
| Stewardship and access | Local authorities can close unsafe pits, protect banks, reserve stone for flood or crossing repair, and require provenance for war remains. Exact office boundaries remain unresolved. |
| Scarcity reason | Suitable accessible stone, transport weight, imported metal supply, fuel, skilled repair labor, and postwar reconstruction constrain availability. War salvage is finite and evidentiary. |
| Regional cultural meaning | Useful stone and metal are valued for maintenance and reliability. A repaired hinge, plow, marker, or bridge fitting can signify fulfilled duty more strongly than ornament, without defining an item catalog. |
| Broad cross-region dependency | Thornfell offers mountain extraction and stonework precedent; Emberholt offers forge and finished-metal precedent. Lowmark's markets and crossings can mediate exchange without proving origin, volume, or technical transfer. |

## Foundation 3 — textile fiber and weaving

| Required field | Proposed foundation |
|---|---|
| World source or import dependency | Farm households grow ordinary textile-fiber crops on suitable drier plots within the existing agricultural landscape, while existing livestock husbandry supplies a smaller fleece or hair stream. Imported fiber or cloth covers poor harvests and specialized needs. No named crop, herd species, grade, or output is approved. Reeds serve bounded household and utility work without being treated as universal cloth. |
| Who gathers or imports it | Farm and household laborers harvest crop fiber; animal-keeping households collect fleece or hair as part of ordinary husbandry; carriers and markets bring outside fiber or cloth. |
| Who processes it | Households perform much sorting, washing, spinning, mending, and simple weaving. Market or village specialists handle shared finishing, larger cloth, and repairs beyond household capacity. |
| Broad processing method | Harvesting or shearing where applicable, cleaning, drying, carding or equivalent preparation, spinning, weaving or felting, cutting, stitching, washing, and repair establish the chain without specifying techniques or goods. |
| Fuel and water needs | Washing and finishing need clean water; drying and some finishing need modest heat. Work must be scheduled so dirty wash water does not return directly to domestic or irrigation channels. |
| Waste and risk | Dust, rot, pests, contaminated wash water, fire in dry stock, repetitive labor injury, and loss of cloth during migration or flood are ordinary risks. |
| Stewardship and access | Households control their labor and stores; shared work spaces answer to local water and fire duties. Market power can affect access, but no textile monopoly is proposed. |
| Scarcity reason | Harvest season, household labor, damaged stores, imported supply, clean drying space, and the time required for spinning and repair create scarcity. |
| Regional cultural meaning | Visible mending and inherited cloth express continuity, grief, migration, and practical pride. Memorial scraps retain custody and provenance; they are not feedstock for ordinary production. |
| Broad cross-region dependency | Thornfell may exchange wool or weather-working knowledge; Emberholt may supply finished colored cloth or demand Lowmark fiber; Mire households may exchange repair practice. None is an exclusive source. |

## Foundation 4 — hide curing and tanning

| Required field | Proposed foundation |
|---|---|
| World source or import dependency | Ordinary livestock and hunting already justify hides and sinew as byproducts. Outside hides may supplement them; no animal, hunting table, or output catalog is selected. |
| Who gathers or imports it | Animal-keeping households, hunters, butchers in the broad occupational sense, carriers, and market traders transfer hides to curing households or small specialists. |
| Who processes it | Initial cleaning and curing can occur near the source; tanning and finishing belong to workers located away from domestic and memorial water. Repairers and households maintain existing leather. |
| Broad processing method | Prompt cleaning, salting or drying in the broad sense, soaking, scraping, tanning with an ordinary agent chosen later, controlled drying, softening, cutting, and repair provide the chain. No reagent or product is approved. |
| Fuel and water needs | Washing and soaking require substantial water; drying and some treatment require modest heat. Water access follows ordinary community duties and C09 boundaries, with no magical shortcut. |
| Waste and risk | Odor, flesh and hair waste, dirty water, vermin, disease, chemical irritation from any later-selected tannage, and conflict over downstream effects make location politically important. |
| Stewardship and access | Animal owners and hunters hold initial claims; local councils or water stewards can restrict disposal and site use; public repair need can influence allocation without erasing ownership. |
| Scarcity reason | Seasonal slaughter or hunting, spoilage before curing, limited clean water, unpleasant labor, safe waste sites, and imported supply constrain output. |
| Regional cultural meaning | Well-maintained leather reflects thrift and responsibility toward the animal and the worker. Wasteful spoilage carries social disapproval in communities accustomed to disrupted harvests. |
| Broad cross-region dependency | Thornfell's goat, hide, and weather-gear precedent allows exchange of hides or knowledge; no exact trade or superior regional technique is asserted. |

## Foundation 5 — clay and ordinary ceramics

| Required field | Proposed foundation |
|---|---|
| World source or import dependency | Suitable common clay can be taken from selected low-impact banks or shallow pits only after local ground is judged stable. Imported clay or finished vessels cover places where extraction would damage water works. Sand, ash, and glass use do not by themselves prove ceramic production. |
| Who gathers or imports it | Seasonal diggers, bank workers, carriers, and market traders supply household or village potters. Water stewards and repair crews have reason to object to unsafe digging. |
| Who processes it | Potters and assistants clean, settle, prepare, shape, dry, fire, inspect, and reclaim ordinary clay where useful. This creates a labor foundation, not a vessel catalog. |
| Broad processing method | Controlled digging, settling or levigation in the broad sense, wedging, forming, slow drying, firing, and sorting cracked work are enough. No clay body, glaze, temperature, or recipe is chosen. |
| Fuel and water needs | Preparation uses water; firing uses accounted fuel. Kiln placement must protect homes, stored harvest, banks, and shared water from smoke and spoil. |
| Waste and risk | Eroded banks, flooded pits, smoke, fire, dust, broken ware, spent kiln material, and dirty settling water are the main costs. |
| Stewardship and access | Councils, landholders, and community water stewards can close a source or firing place that threatens irrigation, bank stability, or household safety. Larger works may require charter oversight only where they affect larger irrigation, consistent with C09. |
| Scarcity reason | Suitable ground, safe extraction windows, drying weather, fuel, skilled firing, breakage, and competing bank uses make ordinary ceramics locally variable. |
| Regional cultural meaning | Repairable, locally witnessed vessels support storage, cooking, medicine, mourning, and hospitality. Their meaning comes from use and custody, not stored emotion. |
| Broad cross-region dependency | Mire clay use and Emberholt firing expertise establish possible exchange contexts; neither proves a direct source or imported technique. Legacy Riverglass remains outside approval. |

## Foundation 6 — ordinary smithing and fuel

| Required field | Proposed foundation |
|---|---|
| World source or import dependency | Lowmark smithing is chiefly repair-oriented and depends on conventional imported metal stock, recoverable civilian scrap, and fuel from managed wood or broad trade. Local primary metal extraction and smelting remain unasserted. |
| Who gathers or imports it | Traders, carriers, farms, crossing crews, militia custodians, and repair offices bring worn tools, fittings, stock, or documented scrap to smiths. |
| Who processes it | Village and market repair smiths maintain agricultural tools, ferry and bridge fittings, household hardware, and ordinary militia equipment. This establishes a social role without an equipment list. |
| Broad processing method | Sorting, cleaning, reheating, reshaping, joining, sharpening, fitting, tempering only in the ordinary broad sense, cooling, and inspection form the chain. No formula or quality tier is selected. |
| Fuel and water needs | Fuel is an accounted claim on managed woodland or imports. Clean water supports cooling and cleaning under ordinary shared-water duties; combat water magic has no role. |
| Waste and risk | Smoke, sparks, fire, hot scale, sharp fragments, slag where applicable, polluted cooling water, and counterfeit war salvage require separation from homes, stores, and channels. |
| Stewardship and access | Smiths judge repairability; owners retain claims; civic or crossing needs may receive priority during emergency through ordinary local authority. Weapons, war remains, and suspicious resonant material require custody rather than casual reuse. |
| Scarcity reason | Imported stock, fuel, skilled labor, repair queues, flood or route disruption, and finite scrap constrain work. Lowmark is not an industrial metal center. |
| Regional cultural meaning | The respected smith keeps necessary work in service and knows when a damaged piece should be retired. Repair supports Lowmark's ethic of patience and continuity without becoming Riverwarden magic. |
| Broad cross-region dependency | Thornfell and Emberholt provide broad smithing and metal precedents. Lowmark supplies food, freight access, and repair demand in return, without fixed contracts or routes. |

## C09 application across the foundations

1. A household may use openly taught, simple water practice in ordinary farming contexts.
2. Experienced community stewards oversee shared water works whose failure affects neighbors.
3. Formal charter oversight applies only to larger irrigation works.
4. Combat water magic remains exclusive to the Last Field-Mage.
5. Forestry, tanning, textiles, clay work, and smithing use ordinary water infrastructure. No process receives magical enhancement, licensing class, market privilege, or player-facing spell system from C09.
