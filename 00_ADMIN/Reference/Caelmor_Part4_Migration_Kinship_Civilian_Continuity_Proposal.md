# Caelmor Part 4 — migration, kinship, and civilian continuity

**Status:** additive proposal for Creative Director review. This document creates no canon and edits no source dossier. It develops the Part 2 historical backbone and Part 3 regional/cross-region proposals into a civilian continuity framework. The Creative Director's rulings through C15 override stale source wording. **Anchored** identifies an existing supported condition; **Directed** identifies a ruling; **Proposed** identifies a connective fact that requires approval before canon use.

## Purpose and limits

Caelmor's regions have been connected by ordinary movement before and after the Great War: food and work, care for displaced people, households divided by necessity, marriages made where people settled, and obligations that outlived the emergency that created them. These are not one hidden network, a universal custom, or proof that every missing record has a Remnant cause.

**Anchored:** Lowmark has river movement, agricultural obligations, lineages, and surviving administrative records; Thornfell has pass, bridge, horn, and clan obligations; the Mire has shifting channels, care and remembrance custodians, and the central human cost of the war; Emberholt has civic/guild institutions, firecraft, and a contested war inheritance. The Part 3 continuity map supports broad ties across every pair but preserves map uncertainty. `Caelmor_Part3_*_Proposal.md`; `Caelmor_Part3_Cross_Region_Continuity_Proposal.md`; `LORE/Factions/Caelmor_Factions.md`.

**Directed:** the Great War lasted 5–10 years and ended about thirty years ago. The present therefore permits living adult witnesses, people who were displaced as children, and adults who know the war through a parent or a record. Do not assign exact ages, dates, parentage, or travel paths without source support. The Mire's earth-memory phenomena, the Stonebound response at Echochamber Summit, dreams, and Remnant activity cannot serve as automatic historical witnesses.

## Continuity method

A cross-region household connection becomes usable only when it has all six parts below. A shared phrase, food, mark, or memorial image is a lead, not a connection.

| Required part | Editorial rule |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Local event or relationship | Identify a concrete evacuation, employment arrangement, care placement, marriage, debt, repair, or return. |
| 2. Experiencer or recorder | Name a role or established anchor who experienced it, or identify the locally appropriate record form. |
| 3. Other-region knowledge | State what the second region actually retained: a household account, roster, charter, repair notation, testimony, or guild account. |
| 4. Difference | The accounts must differ because of timing, duty, incentive, grief, or incomplete access; neither account is made false by default. |
| 5. Independent later evidence | A third source, physical place, or second record can narrow one claim. It cannot be supplied by a speaking Stonebound, an undead witness, or an uncorroborated dream. |
| 6. Reveal owner | One region owns the eventual practical resolution; corroborating regions retain authority over their own records and losses. |

## Movement and settlement patterns

### Before the war

**Proposed:** seasonal and need-based movement connected Lowmark food and river crossings, Thornfell pass labor, Mire channel and care knowledge, and Emberholt civic and guild work. Some households had recurring business or service ties outside their home region, but no source supports a single continuous route, formal continental market, or a shared migration authority.

**Proposed:** interregional marriages, fosterage, and adoption arise most safely at points of repeated civilian contact: a worker remaining after a repair season, a child lodged with known kin during illness or hardship, a bereaved household taking in a relation, or a marriage following long-standing trade/care acquaintance. These are family-specific matters. They do not create transferable regional bloodlines, inherited elemental claims, or a common noble genealogy.

### During the war

**Proposed:** war pressure created staggered civilian movement rather than one single refugee flight. A household could leave because food was requisitioned, a pass closed, a field-care site became unsafe, or a workplace catastrophe displaced its workers. A person may appear in a departure record, an escort or relief record, and a later host household account without any record proving the full journey.

**Proposed:** emergency care and temporary lodging produced informal obligations: a family that received grain later supplied labor; a host household kept a child's name on a list until kin could be found; a guild account treated an outside repairer as a contractor while the repairer's home community remembered a neighbor doing emergency service. Such obligations can survive as disputed favors, not as legal debts enforceable across all regions.

### After the war

**Proposed:** return did not always restore a household's earlier place. Some displaced people stayed where work, marriage, care, or safety made a new life possible; others returned carrying a spouse, foster child, or obligation from elsewhere. A present family story may therefore hold both a local identity and a credible connection to another region without implying that its members are agents, veterans, or custodians of a grand secret.

**Directed:** no durable central authority imposed a universal postwar rule. Any record of resettlement, custody, work, property, or memorial status is governed by the local institution that made it. The same person can be a Lowmark claimant, a Thornfell missing name, a Mire care recipient, or an Emberholt worker in different records without a contradiction unless the dates and direct evidence exclude one another.

## Six pairwise civilian evidence chains

The following are **Proposed** chains. They give every pair a meaningful civilian connection, distinct evidence ownership, and a later correction path. They are alternatives or seeds, not a demand to populate all six with the same family.

### Lowmark ↔ Thornfell — the undelivered relief household

1. **Local event/relationship:** A Lowmark household's relief consignment left local custody during a war closure and was expected by a Thornfell shelter household; the two households had a prewar grain-and-escort obligation.
2. **Experiencer/recorder:** Lowmark's appropriate holder is a freight, mill, or lineage account; Thornfell's is a shelter ledger, horn/closure roster, or bridge-maintenance witness.
3. **Other-region knowledge:** Lowmark knows that the consignment departed; Thornfell knows that it failed to reach the intended household when expected.
4. **Difference:** Lowmark calls its duty discharged at handoff; Thornfell counts the loss at non-arrival. Neither record establishes theft or betrayal.
5. **Independent later evidence:** A repaired bridge mark or a separate civilian arrival account can establish delay and diversion without inventing a precise road.
6. **Reveal/resolution owner:** Thornfell owns the household recognition and any present apology or claim; Lowmark owns correction of the delivery record.

### Lowmark ↔ Mire — the transferred care name

1. **Local event/relationship:** A displaced Lowmark child or dependent was entered into Mire civilian care after a household separation; a later Lowmark household account continued to list the person as missing or presumed dead.
2. **Experiencer/recorder:** A Mire caretaker, testimony register, or burial/care list holds the immediate record; Lowmark retains a household letter, ferry record, or inheritance notation.
3. **Other-region knowledge:** Mire knows that a person was received under a partial or uncertain name; Lowmark knows a person left during upheaval and did not return.
4. **Difference:** Mire preserves uncertainty rather than asserting an identity; Lowmark seeks a definite name to settle household obligations.
5. **Independent later evidence:** A third household's postwar care correspondence, or a matching non-magical personal detail in both records, can narrow the identity. Chapel Shades and the General/Sorrowback cannot identify the person.
6. **Reveal/resolution owner:** Lowmark owns present family and property consequences; the Mire owns the ethical decision to amend, retain, or qualify its testimony.

### Lowmark ↔ Emberholt — the emergency contract that became kin

1. **Local event/relationship:** A Lowmark provider and an Emberholt civic or guild household became connected through a wartime supply contract; after the war, a surviving dependent was hosted, employed, or married into one household with the other's consent.
2. **Experiencer/recorder:** Lowmark holds the contract or household account; Emberholt holds a guild, civic, work, or household record.
3. **Other-region knowledge:** Emberholt remembers needed heat-safety or recovery work; Lowmark remembers the emergency requisition pressure that made the arrangement costly.
4. **Difference:** The same contract reads as civic necessity in Emberholt and as the beginning of a household shortage in Lowmark. The later kin tie does not absolve either institution.
5. **Independent later evidence:** A postwar employment or host-household notation can distinguish voluntary settlement from a wartime confiscation without imputing Remnant involvement.
6. **Reveal/resolution owner:** Lowmark owns the disputed obligation; Emberholt owns whether public/guild credit accurately names outside help and the settled household.

### Thornfell ↔ Mire — the escort and the fostered survivor

1. **Local event/relationship:** A Thornfell pass closure separated a civilian group; one survivor arrived in Mire care and was fostered locally while kin remained uncertain in Thornfell.
2. **Experiencer/recorder:** Thornfell retains a closure, horn, cairn, or escort record; the Mire retains care testimony, a host-household account, or a memorial note.
3. **Other-region knowledge:** Thornfell knows the group was lost from its custody; the Mire knows an unnamed or partially named survivor lived beyond the closure.
4. **Difference:** Thornfell's cairn records loss at the last confirmed moment; the Mire's record preserves survival without proving a complete kin link.
5. **Independent later evidence:** A Lowmark departure copy, civilian relief notation, or a shared non-ritual object description may supply chronology. Earth-memory does not act as testimony.
6. **Reveal/resolution owner:** Thornfell owns the kin recognition and cairn correction; the Mire owns the foster household's account and whether it releases sensitive care testimony.

### Thornfell ↔ Emberholt — the repair partnership and broken marriage bargain

1. **Local event/relationship:** A prewar or early-war repair partnership joined a Thornfell bridge/building household and an Emberholt civic/guild household. A planned marriage, completed marriage, or shared guardianship then came under strain when access and safety terms changed.
2. **Experiencer/recorder:** Thornfell keeps a work obligation, clan acknowledgement, or pass closure testimony; Emberholt keeps a guild repair account or household correspondence.
3. **Other-region knowledge:** Thornfell remembers an Emberholt partner as a specific worker or relation, not as the precursor; Emberholt remembers that access was denied or conditions imposed on a necessary repair.
4. **Difference:** Thornfell treats refusal of military or unsafe access as duty of care; Emberholt may remember it as abandonment of a contracted relationship. A family breach need not be ideological.
5. **Independent later evidence:** Material repair evidence and a separately dated guild account can establish joint work, while still leaving the marriage/guardianship choice personal. Echochamber's Stonebound response cannot certify a participant's history.
6. **Reveal/resolution owner:** Thornfell owns the pass-duty judgment; Emberholt owns public correction of guild credit and the household's current standing.

### Mire ↔ Emberholt — the workplace displacement and divided memorial

1. **Local event/relationship:** An Emberholt workplace disaster or war escalation displaced a civilian household, some of whom reached Mire care or married into a Mire host family after the immediate crisis.
2. **Experiencer/recorder:** Emberholt retains a civic or guild loss account; the Mire retains a refugee/care testimony, host-household record, or memorial practice.
3. **Other-region knowledge:** Emberholt knows who was absent from a workplace or civic roll; the Mire knows whom it received and how that person described the loss.
4. **Difference:** Emberholt's public account may use a safety or workplace frame; Mire testimony holds the human cost and uncertainty. Neither makes every omission deliberate concealment.
5. **Independent later evidence:** A contemporary personal letter, outside service record, or verifiable date at a surviving place can correct one casualty assumption. Restricted records may matter in a specific case, but Remnant involvement requires its own evidence.
6. **Reveal/resolution owner:** The Mire owns care testimony and the host family's memory; Emberholt owns the civic memorial and any correction of the official account.

## Multi-generation household arcs beyond the immediate war

These five **Proposed** arcs provide recurring civilian continuity without turning one family into a continental key. Each uses a different household, region pair, and cause of connection. “Generation” here means a relationship carried from an older household into a later one; it does not assign an exact age, surname, or genealogy to any existing anchor.

### Lowmark ↔ Thornfell — the prewar bridge marriage

**Origin:** Before the war, a Thornfell bridge-maintenance household and a Lowmark crossing-account household maintained a recurring professional relationship. A marriage joined one member of each household after years of practical acquaintance, while each partner retained duties in their home place.

1. **Local event or relationship:** The marriage formalized an existing bridge-and-crossing obligation; periodic visits concern accounts and seasonal maintenance, not a fixed cross-country route.
2. **Experiencer or recorder:** Thornfell retains a clan acknowledgement or maintenance obligation; Lowmark retains a household/lineage notation or crossing account.
3. **Other-region knowledge:** Each household knows the other as an in-law household with an old work tie, rather than as a foreign abstraction.
4. **Difference:** Thornfell treats the marriage as joining people while leaving pass duty local; Lowmark treats it as a relationship that carried a shared crossing responsibility. Neither view creates jurisdiction over the other place.
5. **Independent later evidence:** Matching dates in a repair account and a household remembrance can confirm the relationship's working context without requiring an exact ceremony date or travel itinerary.
6. **Reveal/resolution owner:** Thornfell owns the bridge obligation; Lowmark owns any household claim attached to the crossing account.

**Present consequence:** Descendants or later household members disagree over whether an inherited promise requires a present repair contribution. The answer can be a limited local settlement, with no need to turn marriage into a political alliance.

### Thornfell ↔ Mire — fosterage after a war separation

**Origin:** During the war, a child separated from a Thornfell civilian group entered a Mire host household's care. The host household later treated the child as family; any formal adoption status remains unselected until a local-law pass supports it.

1. **Local event or relationship:** Emergency fosterage became a durable household relationship after return to known Thornfell kin remained unsafe, impossible, or unconfirmed.
2. **Experiencer or recorder:** Thornfell has the group/closure record; the Mire has the caretaker's testimony and host-household account.
3. **Other-region knowledge:** Thornfell preserves the child as a missing relation or a loss with uncertainty; the Mire preserves the child's life after arrival and the host household's care duty.
4. **Difference:** Thornfell wants a definitive restoration of kin identity; the Mire resists describing care as temporary when the host household did the work of raising the survivor.
5. **Independent later evidence:** A later letter, neutral relief notation, or a prewar household detail in two records can narrow the connection. The Sorrowback, General, Chapel Shades, and other earth-memory phenomena supply no identity evidence.
6. **Reveal/resolution owner:** The Mire owns the care account and the adult survivor's privacy; Thornfell owns its missing-name and cairn practice.

**Present consequence:** A later generation may inherit two legitimate household histories and face a memorial question: whether an old Thornfell loss is amended, while the Mire household's parentage and care are fully acknowledged.

### Lowmark ↔ Mire — postwar return and elder-care obligation

**Origin:** A Lowmark displaced adult who had received Mire care returned after the war, but an older Mire caregiver later required help when their own household could no longer manage alone. The prior recipient's Lowmark household accepted an elder-care obligation through correspondence or a returning relation.

1. **Local event or relationship:** Care given during displacement becomes a postwar responsibility carried back to a Lowmark household; the relationship is gratitude and kin-like duty, not proof of blood relation.
2. **Experiencer or recorder:** The Mire has the original care testimony and a host-household account; Lowmark has a letter, hosting notation, field/household account, or witness to the later arrangement.
3. **Other-region knowledge:** Mire knows why the caregiver's household sought aid; Lowmark knows the older person's practical needs and the earlier help received.
4. **Difference:** The Mire may describe the obligation as communal care returned; Lowmark may describe it as a specific household debt. Neither record alone sets a general rule for care claims.
5. **Independent later evidence:** A third-party correspondence, a dated seasonal account, or a neutral visitor's record can establish that the obligation was carried out without fixing an unsupported travel path.
6. **Reveal/resolution owner:** Lowmark owns the present accommodation or household claim; the Mire owns interpretation of the original caregiver's service and whether its care record is shared.

**Present consequence:** The older person's death, departure, or changing needs can reopen a practical dispute over who bears continuing care, while clarifying that a remembered “debt” began as survival work rather than a secret compact.

### Lowmark ↔ Emberholt — non-war craft/service dependency

**Origin:** Before the war and again after it, a Lowmark household that kept crossings or records relied on periodic Emberholt civic/guild service for a technical inspection, repair, or safety assessment, while the Emberholt household depended on Lowmark's local access and honest accounts. The exact service, method, and material are deliberately unselected.

1. **Local event or relationship:** Repeated civilian service created a reciprocal professional obligation independent of wartime supply or grain.
2. **Experiencer or recorder:** Lowmark preserves a crossing/household/claim notation; Emberholt preserves a guild or civic work account.
3. **Other-region knowledge:** Lowmark knows the workers as people trusted to assess a recurring local problem; Emberholt knows the Lowmark household as the local custodian who could authorize access and verify results.
4. **Difference:** Lowmark treats the work as a local safety duty; Emberholt treats it as skilled civic labor deserving credit or payment. Later disagreement can concern cost, access, or credit without becoming a fire-magic dispute.
5. **Independent later evidence:** A surviving repair feature and a separately held payment, authorization, or correspondence record can corroborate the service. It does not prove an exact route or that a modern guild inherited the original household's authority.
6. **Reveal/resolution owner:** Lowmark owns the safety/access decision; Emberholt owns professional attribution and any guild claim.

**Present consequence:** A present repair dispute can expose that current households have been mutually dependent longer than either public account admits, while leaving both regions free to contest terms.

### Mire ↔ Emberholt — postwar host family and reciprocal apprenticeship care

**Origin:** A civilian displaced from Emberholt lived with a Mire host household after the war. In adulthood, that person arranged nonexclusive work instruction or care for a younger member of the host household through an Emberholt civic/guild connection. This is a household-specific act of return, not a named apprenticeship system.

1. **Local event or relationship:** Postwar hosting became an enduring inter-household obligation, later answered through access to ordinary civilian instruction, work, or care.
2. **Experiencer or recorder:** Mire retains host-household testimony and a care or departure note; Emberholt retains a household correspondence, workplace/guild notation, or civic acknowledgement.
3. **Other-region knowledge:** The Mire knows the displaced person as a hosted survivor; Emberholt knows that person as a settled worker or neighbor who vouched for an outside household.
4. **Difference:** Mire sees the later assistance as repayment for shelter; Emberholt treats it as an individual recommendation within local standards. Neither account makes the work placement guaranteed or creates an open migration channel.
5. **Independent later evidence:** A third-party household letter, surviving service record, or physical worksite acknowledgement can establish that assistance occurred. A civic memorial or earth-memory manifestation cannot establish motive by itself.
6. **Reveal/resolution owner:** The Mire owns the host family's account of what was owed; Emberholt owns the standards and credit attached to the later instruction or care.

**Present consequence:** A younger household member's contested claim to recognition can uncover an ordinary chain of refuge, hospitality, and returned assistance, while keeping the individual's own merit and present Emberholt standards intact.

## Family and obligation safeguards

| Continuity-safe use | Limit that preserves canon |
| --- | --- |
| A household can have a displaced grandparent, foster relation, spouse, work partner, or correspondent from another region. | Do not assign exact generations, ages, surnames, or genealogy to an existing character without source support. |
| An existing regional anchor can hold a record, witness a present claim, or know a household custom. | Do not presume that an anchor personally fought in the Great War, traveled between regions, or met another named anchor. |
| Marriage and adoption can explain belonging across a border. | Do not use them to prove blood-based magical inheritance, political entitlement, or access to hidden sites. |
| A postwar obligation can cause a present disagreement. | It remains local unless both jurisdictions have a record; it does not establish a cross-region court or treaty. |
| A marriage, fosterage, elder-care tie, or service relationship can continue through later households. | Each arc needs its own evidence; do not merge them into a single line of descent or assume a regional custom from one household. |
| A record can be incomplete, protective, or self-serving. | Missing paper alone is not evidence of the Remnant, supernatural interference, or intentional fraud. |

## Editorial readiness check

The six chains cover all regional pairs through different civilian causes: food/escort, care/identity, supply/settlement, fosterage, repair/household obligation, and workplace displacement. Each retains a local owner and independent correction path. None relies on motif similarity, an exact map claim, speaking Stonebound, literal undead, or the Remnant as universal explanation.

Before a chain is made canon or used in later authored content, the relevant worker must choose only the facts needed for that case: the household(s), local record, precise evidence, and whether a named existing anchor actually belongs. Map review must verify any placed travel path. Until then, these are deliberately bounded proposals.
