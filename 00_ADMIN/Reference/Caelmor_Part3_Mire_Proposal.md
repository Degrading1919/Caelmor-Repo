# Caelmor Part 3 — Mire regional expansion

**Status:** proposal for Creative Director review. This document does not alter canon, design equipment or resources, or script quests. It develops a lived regional brief from the detailed Mire dossier, current factions and character anchors, Stage A, and the approved Part 2 proposal set (commit `8c8bb9d`). It treats commit `4d2d6b6` as quarantined source material only.

## Reading key and decision boundary

- **Directed** — a Creative Director ruling recorded in Part 2 or Stage A.
- **Anchored** — supported by current canon, cited in prose.
- **Proposed** — a connected addition for approval, never a silent canon change.
- **Open** — withheld pending a Director choice.

**Directed C14:** the region's central flooded chapel is one location with three names: the formal and historical **Chapel of the Sinking Star**, the common modern **Sunken Chapel**, and the riverfolk and ferrymen's **Sunken Church**. Rowan Mirewake is the only known living guide who reliably knows the safe approach through its shifting channels. The origin of “Sinking Star” is not established here. **Directed C15:** the Mire has exactly two major supernatural threats: the General Who Wouldn't Fall and the Sorrowback. Neither is undead. Other rare, place-bound memory phenomena remain local. `Caelmor_Stage_A_Contradiction_Register.md`, C14–C15; Creative Director rulings.

**Directed C08:** “spirit” is an older term for the living and emotional imprint people leave in the world, and for the interaction of memory, grief, intention, and identity with elemental resonance. It is neither a fifth element nor a separate magical discipline. **Directed C12:** postwar authorities broadly rejected wartime weaponization, amplification, uncontrolled ritual experimentation, and dangerous research, but no durable authority imposed a universal magical ban. `Caelmor_Part2_Historical_Backbone_Proposal.md` (8c8bb9d), Reading key and Magic-history framework.

## The regional proposition

**Anchored:** The Mire is a low country of raised homes, channels, war remains, earth-memory phenomena, communal mourning, and difficult crossings. Sorrowmere, Marshfall, Griefwatch, and Rootrest already give it distinct human centers; Memorykeepers, Chapel-Tenders, Marshwardens, Rootcrafters, and Bannerbearers already give it civic work. `LORE/Regions/Caelmor_Region3_MireOfFallenBanners.md`, I–VIII.

**Proposed:** The Mire is not defined by grief alone. It is a place where people have developed public ways to decide what must be carried together, what must be repaired before the next rain, and what a family may keep private. Its political argument is not whether the war matters, but who has the right to interpret the dead, close a crossing, demand labor, or call an old obligation due. This makes the Mire's emotional weight a present social condition rather than a museum atmosphere.

## Historical inheritance

### Before the war: ground, testimony, and local duty

**Anchored:** Memorywardens were earth-attuned caretakers who connected soil, grief, memory, and stability. Their work included healing erosion, stabilizing gatherings, and teaching remembrance without collapse; modern Chapel-Tenders and Memorykeepers retain forms but no active practice. `LORE/Regions/Caelmor_Region3_MireOfFallenBanners.md`, IV–V, XIII.

**Proposed:** Before rediscovery, raised banks, ferries, and named safe ground made cooperation necessary. A household could retain use-right to a bank or mooring, but its standing rested on keeping it usable for others in flood season. The later Memorywarden ethic made this legible: testimony precedes placement, no family is made to carry a communal loss alone, and no site should be forced to bear more grief than its people can tend. These were civic safeguards as well as an earth tradition; present people can observe their forms without practicing magic.

**Proposed:** The Memorywardens did not govern the whole Mire. They taught custodians and received requests at difficult sites; boat households, bank families, forest-edge workers, and travelling recorders retained practical authority. This prevents the old order from becoming a vanished state and explains why present rites vary by settlement. A Rootrest marker might prioritize a living caregiver's account; a Sorrowmere list might preserve three conflicting names; Griefwatch might record a place before a person because patrol safety came first.

### The Great War: the land as a crowded witness

**Directed / Anchored:** The Great War lasted five to ten years and ended about thirty years before the present. The Mire was its central battlefield: trenches, siege lines, flooded fields, camps, pyres, and displaced households left lasting physical and emotional damage. Memorywardens were conscripted as guides and healers; overwhelming trauma broke their practices. `Caelmor_Part2_War_and_Postwar_Proposal.md` (8c8bb9d); `LORE/Regions/Caelmor_Region3_MireOfFallenBanners.md`, II, IV.

**Proposed:** The war converted local duties into emergency work. Boat crews carried wounded people and contradictory orders; families surrendered sleeping space to strangers; high ground became a watch site, treatment yard, or mass burial boundary. The same raised paths that once connected households became decisions about which people could reach shelter. This is why a modern argument over a path repair is also an argument about inherited access and remembered abandonment.

**Proposed:** The order's collapse was not one single magical catastrophe. Some Memorywardens died in service, some refused military demands, and some found their bounded practices unequal to trenches, pyres, and mass burial. Their survivors did not leave an intact secret lineage. A chapel form, a soil-touch before testimony, or a stone circle can be an inherited social act without secretly functioning as a spell. Earth-memory impressions are partial and place-bound; they do not provide an objective recording that settles human testimony.

### Recovery and the unsettled peace

**Directed:** The postwar agreement rejected dangerous wartime magical practices but regional enforcement varied; no universal ban existed. `Caelmor_Part2_Historical_Backbone_Proposal.md` (8c8bb9d), The Stopping and Repair Bargains.

**Proposed:** Mire communities interpreted that agreement as a ban on making grief, ground, or the dead serve military purpose. They did not outlaw mourning, chapel vigils, or environmental observation. This explains a culture suspicious of anyone who offers a clean use for a war site, whether a visitor calls it research, salvage, pilgrimage, or public order. It also lets residents disagree: some see recovered Memorywarden marks as dangerous things to leave alone, some as evidence needed to correct names, and some as a source of badly needed income.

**Proposed:** Recovery created authority from repetition. Whoever organized a ferry roster, raised a lantern after storm damage, or received an unclaimed body became necessary before they became official. That authority remains distributed and fragile; its legitimacy is tested every wet season.

## Present government and competing interests

**Anchored:** Current government is fragmented among marsh communities, chapel councils, and cooperatives. `LORE/Factions/Caelmor_Factions.md`, regional governments; `LORE/Regions/Caelmor_Region3_MireOfFallenBanners.md`, III, VIII.

**Proposed operating model:** no council rules the region. Settlements recognize a shared need to keep routes, remains, and emergency stores from becoming private property. The following practices offer a usable political layer, subject to Director approval:

| Practice | Present purpose | Pressure point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Names Below register** | Recovered remains are listed by place, witness, identifying signs, and uncertainty before wartime allegiance. Open readings let families contest or add testimony. | A family wants a heroic identity; a banner worker refuses to invent one; a visitor wants a recovered name removed. |
| **Reedsward** | Households rotate maintenance of paths, ferry moorings, lantern stakes, and warning posts. It is civic labor, not a militia. | A wealthier household buys substitutes; an exhausted caregiver misses a shift; a settlement claims a path serves outsiders more than its own children. |
| **Silt hearing** | A small hearing of affected households, one Chapel-Tender, and one Memorykeeper mediates disturbed ground, route claims, recovered property, and old rescue obligations. | Its force depends on cooperation; witnesses can be pressured and a judgment can be ignored by someone with a boat or money. |
| **Lantern debt** | Rescue, shelter, or route work is recorded as a relationship to be acknowledged, not a price that can erase care. | A creditor treats it as hereditary debt; a young adult refuses to inherit a wartime obligation; a visitor mistakes hospitality for consent to exploitation. |

**Proposed interests:** Sorrowmere's public tenders seek dependable record and equal access; Marshfall households want flexible route and harvest decisions; Griefwatch insists on closures around unsafe ground; Rootrest resists decisions that turn healing sites into public spectacle. Ferrymen protect judgment to refuse a bad crossing. Banner and bone tenders resist sorting the dead into convenient political stories. Memorykeepers want records legible enough to correct them. None is uniformly virtuous: the same people can protect an unclaimed child, hoard a dry berth, suppress an embarrassing testimony, or keep a stranger alive.

## Households and the rhythm of a life

**Proposed:** A Mire household is often a work group before it is a bloodline: a ferrier and a marker-setter may share a roof with a widowed sibling, an apprentice, a fostered child, and an older neighbor who can no longer walk wet paths safely. This is not exceptional generosity; war losses, postwar migration, and seasonal work made flexible households durable. It also creates friction over whose testimony counts, who owes Reedsward labor, and whether someone who left still has a claim on a room or name.

- **Childhood:** children learn depth marks, which light means wait, how to carry a message without promising a crossing, and the difference between repeating a story and claiming it as fact. A missing relative is not made into a bedtime ghost. Children may leave a small sign at an unmarked second place during a mourning meal; adults read an abandoned sign as a request to ask after someone, not proof of death.
- **Work and class:** dry berths, boat ownership, records literacy, and the ability to pay a substitute for communal labor separate households more sharply than noble title. A ferry household may be respected and still be one storm away from debt. A Memorykeeper's access to paper and travel gives influence; a peat worker's knowledge of ground gives leverage that is easy for records to erase.
- **Migration:** some families left for Lowmark farms, Thornfell highsteads, or Emberholt wage work after the war. Their return can be a homecoming, a claim for housing, or an accusation that they bring outside priorities. Newcomers may become kin through work before they are trusted with memorial obligations.
- **Aging and grief:** elders hold route memory but can be overruled by younger workers who must bear the physical risk. Grief is public enough to receive food, work relief, and witnesses, but privacy remains a value: no one is obliged to tell a whole story to earn care.
- **Celebration:** after the first reliable dry stretch, settlements hold a *lifting day* in which a repaired route is walked at ordinary pace, children carry harmless work signs, and households eat before formal remembrance. It celebrates continuity, new apprentices, marriages, births, and returns as well as the dead. A community that cannot celebrate eventually turns every arrival into a funeral.

## Belief, superstition, and magic

**Anchored:** Chapel-Tenders are religious rather than magical; Memorykeepers are civilian recorders; modern Mirefolk do not recognize the Memorywardens as an active order. `LORE/Regions/Caelmor_Region3_MireOfFallenBanners.md`, III–V, XIII.

**Proposed:** Chapel faith is a practice of witness and care. Tenders maintain lantern lines, burial spaces, reflection pools, and meals because people need a place to bring names without turning a place into proof. Some households speak of the Forgotten God's withdrawal as a silence to endure; others use no god-language and call the same act “leaving room.” Neither position gives a character privileged knowledge of the world.

**Directed C08 applied:** older people may call an unusually heavy place “full of spirit,” but mean the imprint of lives and intention meeting earth resonance. A banner shifting, a trench humming, or animals avoiding a ford can be a local anomaly, practical warning, grief response, or more than one of these. It never establishes a spirit element, a soul economy, or a person literally returned from death.

### The Sunken Chapel and the Mire's two major threats

**Directed C14:** the Chapel of the Sinking Star is the Sunken Chapel in common modern speech and the Sunken Church to riverfolk and ferrymen; all three names identify the same flooded site. Rowan Mirewake is the only known living guide who reliably knows its safe approach through shifting channels. The name's origin remains unasserted.

**Directed C15:** the General Who Wouldn't Fall is a singular, unusually coherent earth-memory manifestation concentrated from battlefield command, fear, obedience, death, and trauma. It is not the surviving ghost of any historical general. The Sorrowback is the Mire's other major threat: a massive earth-memory creature formed from soil, violence, broken weapons, banners, and accumulated emotional residue. Neither is undead, and neither makes the dead literally walk.

**Directed C15 applied:** Earthen Echoes, Banner Wraiths, Chapel Shades, and other rare place-bound memory manifestations are minor or local phenomena. Chapel Shades are memory impressions, not souls. A reflection that appears too clear, a soil tremor during testimony, or a banner moved by no felt wind can remain an environmental or resonant warning, but no anomaly should answer a case cleanly. Caelmor's world can remember without its dead literally walking around.

**Proposed consequence chain:** because the Sunken Chapel's approach shifts, route safety depends on Rowan's judgment rather than a route anyone can certify once and treat as permanent. A ferryman may rely on Rowan's warning and still refuse passage; a Silt hearing can record that refusal, its weather and safety evidence, and the effects on affected households without making chapel access a private right. When the General or the Sorrowback disturbs ground or crossings, Griefwatch may call a closure and Memorykeepers may separate observed effects from claims about what happened there. The General, the Sorrowback, and a Chapel Shade cannot identify a historical event, settle testimony, or decide whose loss carries civic authority.

These sites should support environmental observation and civilian evidence before any central supernatural revelation. None requires every discrepancy to be Remnant work; shame, ordinary error, deliberate protection, and political convenience remain distinct possible causes.

## Side-story network

**Proposed, not quest scripts:**

1. **A Banner With No Side:** Calwen receives a Thornfell-stitched banner repaired in a Lowmark manner. The immediate dispute is ownership and retirement. Later evidence suggests a mixed treatment crew, while leaving the exact history incomplete.
2. **The Empty Place:** a child leaves a work sign at the second place in a family meal. Adults assume a search for a missing relative; ferry schedules point instead to a living adult who migrated and stopped writing. The family must decide what welcome looks like.
3. **The Bell Roster:** missed calls at Griefwatch are blamed on an aging volunteer. Neighbors had privately changed duties to protect a caregiver and failed to update the public roster. A visiting Thornfell Warden recognizes the need for an accountable substitute rule.
4. **Borrowed Winter:** a relief list links Lowmark grain and Thornfell labor to a Mire household. Present descendants dispute whether help made a debt, a gift, or an obligation to provide equivalent care to strangers.
5. **The Hushed Granary Key:** an emergency key is gone before flood season. The resolution tests whether a rule that saved people after the war can remain humane when a family cannot wait for a hearing.
6. **The Returning Wage:** an Emberholt worker returns to care for an elder. Their wages and official account of the war make them useful and suspect at the same table; the story concerns belonging, not proof of Emberholt conspiracy.
