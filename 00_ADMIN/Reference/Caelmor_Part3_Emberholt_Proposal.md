**Status:** proposal for Creative Director review. This is an additive regional brief, not a canon rewrite, content script, equipment plan, or readiness claim. It reads the approved Stage A work and the Part 2 historical backbone together with the detailed Emberholt dossier, corrected Factions material, timeline, war history, and Emberholt character anchors. ## Reading key and boundaries

- **Directed** — a Creative Director ruling recorded in Stage A or the approved Part 2 work.
- **Anchored** — supported by the current detailed dossier, Factions, timeline, war history, or character-anchor file named below.
- **Proposed** — a connected addition for review. It is not a settled historical fact.
- **Open** — deliberately unchosen. A rumor, an old inconsistent line, or a useful story hook does not decide it.

**Directed limits.** The Great War lasted 5–10 years and ended about 30 years ago. A wartime precursor, rather than the later formal Remnant, conducted the catastrophe that made the Fire Titan; the Titan was a precursor/Remnant initiate, not Volkhari. Kaelith Veyr’un escaped and remains free. Thalan Veyr is the sole current captive. The current Flameward Speaker is male. Spirit is an old name for living and emotional imprint interacting with elemental resonance, not an element or a discipline. Postwar authorities rejected weaponization, amplification, uncontrolled ritual experimentation, and dangerous research, but no durable central authority imposed a universal ban. Emberholt can therefore sustain public civic firecraft under a regional safety regime. Exact Volkhari population (C06), precursor name and formal-Remnant date (C03), and Stonebound agency (C11) remain open. Nothing here determines Mire C14/C15.

## The regional proposition: a city that made safety public

**Anchored:** Emberholt is a volcanic civic center where embercraft appears in lighting, heat, craft, school practice, and public ceremony. It has a public Council, Emberguard, Covenant, forgeguilds, merchant houses, and Cinderwall networks. It also contains the Glassed Hollow, Emberdeep warrens, and hidden Remnant activity. Most citizens can sincerely value the public order while hidden interests exploit it.

**Proposed:** Emberholt's distinctive present tension is not simply “a magical city concealing evil.” It is a city whose people learned, through family stories and scars, that heat can keep a household alive and that uncontrolled fire can destroy a district. Postwar civic institutions earn legitimacy by making useful heat dependable: maintaining vent routes, teaching children to stop a flare, investigating burns, and ensuring shift workers can cool down safely. The same habits create records, tests, access points, and moments of emotional exposure that the formal Remnant can misuse. A household may defend civic embercraft against outsiders while resisting the people who turn it into a means of selection or coercion.

This produces three durable arguments:

1. **Care or control:** when does an inspection, lesson, or safety record stop serving the resident and begin serving an unaccountable authority?
2. **Repair or silence:** can the city honor the Great War and the Glassed Hollow without publicly naming its culpability?
3. **Usefulness or complicity:** can guild work, trade, and civic defense remain honorable when their contracts and data can be diverted below the city?

## Historical inheritance made local

### Before the Great War: the obligation to keep heat safe

**Anchored:** Volkhari lived near the volcanic ridges before the catastrophe; their fire attunement is distinct from learned human embercraft. Stonebound maintenance works lie beneath future Emberholt, but their agency is open. Human embercraft developed into formal study beside older volcanic communities, while civic and guild authority formed around a dangerous lived environment.

**Proposed:** Before formal schools, ridge households kept practical “vent duties”: clear the marked route, keep water and names at a shelter, leave a warning for the next household, and do not enter a heat pocket alone. These are civic survival habits, not proof of a unified ancient religion or a Stonebound command. Human workers, Volkhari neighbors, and later guild records remember different portions of the same terrain. A human account calls an old marked recess a forge precaution; a Volkhari account may recognize a place where families negotiated passage; a Remnant survey treats it as an access problem.

As Emberholt became a larger city, the public offices that coordinated vents, furnaces, and streets gained standing because they could prevent ordinary calamities. The later Speaker's office may have inherited, recast, or displaced that authority; its exact office history is **Open**. This proposal does not choose it. The useful inheritance is that legitimacy rests on visible maintenance, not on claimed bloodline or prophecy.

### The Great War: a civic wound rather than distant legend

**Directed and anchored:** Emberholt's wartime escalation included weaponization and amplification of fire practice. The Glassed Hollow was a coercive catastrophe, later covered publicly as a forge collapse. Kaelith escaped; Thalan was taken afterward; the Fire Titan resulted from an initiate's overload. War History's Volkhari-Titan account and the detailed dossier's long-war chronology conflict with the Director's rulings and are not used as alternate truth.

**Proposed:** War pressure altered the city's social contract. Guild schedules became requisitions. Apprentices were assessed for utility rather than readiness. Families learned the meaning of a sealed work order or a missing shift bell. The most painful surviving divide is between people who remember civic organization as what kept neighbors fed and housed, and people whose families experienced it as the channel through which labor, kin, or records disappeared.

The Glassed Hollow should be felt first through this ordinary inheritance: a grandmother who refuses to use the official word “collapse”; a forge family whose memorial has no body; a district rule that says never to seal a family notice without a witness. Later evidence may disclose coercive experimentation and Volkhari erasure. The proposal does not make every fire accident part of the Hollow, or every bereaved family a keeper of its secret.

### Recovery and the region's law of restraint

**Directed:** After the war, surviving authorities broadly agreed to stop the dangerous wartime practices. The later shorthand that “magic was outlawed” is historically inaccurate. Emberholt interprets restraint through public standards of heat, consent, supervision, and public accountability, while covert actors evade those standards.

**Proposed local transition:**

| Period | Public need | Institution gaining standing | Present argument |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Immediate stopping | Put out unstable fires, reopen safe streets, find missing workers, and separate ordinary craft from wartime methods. | District crews, healers, guild foremen, and surviving guards. | A family demands a record of what happened to a worker; an official calls the same record a source of panic. |
| Repair bargains | Share heat, labor, and safe passage while food and housing remain scarce. | Neighborhood ward stewards, forgeguild mutual aid, and civic courts. | Lower wards say they rebuilt the city but are still inspected as risks. |
| Managed memory | Make public firecraft respectable while containing fear of recurrence. | Speaker, Council, school system, Covenant, and archive offices. | Safety language is protective, but it can conceal dangerous research and historical responsibility. |
| Present unsettled peace | Keep public embercraft useful amid hidden failures, recruitment, and resurfacing records. | Competing civic, guild, guard, religious, merchant, and neighborhood actors. | The question is who gets to define “safe,” and who can challenge that definition. |

The formal Remnant's eventual consolidation belongs in the managed-memory period, but its precursor's name, continuity, and founding date are **Open C03**. The proposal does not attach every postwar compromise to a single clandestine organization.

## Government as a contested daily system

**Anchored:** The Flameward Speaker and Civic Council administer public life; the Emberguard handles city defense and response; the Covenant shapes public belief; guilds and merchant houses hold economic influence; Cinderwall networks protect and exploit lower-ward residents. The Speaker is male by direction, despite stale female language in the detailed dossier.

**Proposed:** The Council should be understood through cases that residents bring to it: heat damage to a rented room, a school placement appeal, a late guild wage, a blocked freight route, a dangerous vent, or a family seeking a missing relative. Its power is real but bounded by whether a district can make its decisions work. This keeps the Council from becoming either a powerless façade or an all-knowing regime.

| Forgeguilds and workers | useful labor, apprenticeships, sound infrastructure, craft dignity | covert contracts, unsafe quotas, and elite extraction of old knowledge | Durnan Volkryst can oppose a contract that resembles wartime work while Selice Morra faces pressure to keep shops open. |
| Merchant houses | food, employment, and workable links beyond the mountain | embargo, disrupted routes, and public scandal | Eraven Cyl's freight can be both necessary to households and compromised by hidden carriage. |

No faction has a monopoly on decency or guilt. A civic rule can protect a child; a guild can hide a dangerous practice; a neighborhood network can preserve a family while exploiting a desperate neighbor. The Remnant is a consequential hidden pressure, not the explanation for every bad judgment, forged record, or class dispute.

### Work, class, migration, and the household life cycle

**Proposed:** Emberholt is a city where heat changes the rhythm of a day, yet it should not turn every resident into a symbolic flame-user. Most people know their neighborhood through shift changes, school routes, rent, fatigue, shared meals, repair queues, and who answers when a vent coughs at night.

- **Childhood:** early embercraft instruction is civic literacy: light, stop, call for help, and do not imitate a stressed adult. Children turn ash marks, cooled glass, and stairway games into folklore. A child with an unreliable response is first a family concern and school concern, before any larger plot. The Quiet Annex seed below can expose unfair exclusion without declaring every unusual child a magical chosen figure.
- **Young adulthood:** apprenticeship, domestic service, guard work, carrying, shop labor, scholarship, and migration offer divergent status. A family may celebrate a first guild wage and fear the obligations that accompany it. Interdistrict marriage or cohabitation carries practical negotiations about heat access, care for elders, and which house hosts a cooling vigil.
- **Middle age:** many residents become the people who translate institutions for others: a foreman explains a safety case number, a Covenant neighbor brings food after a burn, a gate clerk tells a migrant where an appeal can be made. This informal labor is a source of power and resentment.
- **Aging and grief:** workers with old burns, breath damage, or trauma from fires may be honored publicly for endurance while privately losing work. Lowering a household flame during mourning creates a visible invitation for neighbors to bring food, labor, or silence. It must remain voluntary; a household that keeps its flame bright is not presumed uncaring.
- **Celebration:** First Cooling, a proposed annual recovery remembrance, would pair lowered household flames with repaired work shown in public. The Speaker can frame it as renewal; families can use the same evening to name what official accounts omit. Street meals, supervised flame weaving, and a child's first safe ignition give joy a place beside the political memory.

## Belief, “spirit,” and attitudes to embercraft

**Anchored:** The Ember-Seared Covenant treats fire as memory, discipline, and transformation. Embercraft responds to emotional conditions; public instruction emphasizes control. Hearthveil influence and seeded visions are an existing hidden risk.

**Directed C08 applied:** residents may call the felt aftermath of a home, loss, or promise its “spirit,” but the word means a living and emotional imprint interacting with resonance. It does not identify a fifth element, a separate Emberholt priesthood, or an independent magic school.

**Proposed:** Emberholt belief has at least four overlapping registers:

1. **Civic doctrine:** fire requires attention; heat is shared responsibility; emotional discipline prevents harm. Many citizens hold this sincerely because it makes a precarious city livable.
2. **Household practice:** a mourning flame is lowered; a repaired threshold is acknowledged; relatives check a young person's first difficult shift. These acts are care and memory, with no requirement that they produce an effect.
3. **Volkhari memory:** human accounts should not absorb Volkhari inherited fire discipline into civic Emberholt tradition. Rumored cooling, naming, and restraint practices can be encountered as a living culture with its own authority, only after the Director resolves how its surviving population is represented.
4. **Dissent and superstition:** some people fear certain vents, no longer trust a public rite, or interpret an induced dream as divine instruction. A vision is evidence of experience, not automatic proof of its cause. Seralynd's unscripted doubts and Serix Arden's unexpected visions can create a factual inquiry without settling Dreamer metaphysics.

## Story networks rooted in people and institutions

The following are **proposed side-story networks**, not quest scripts. Each begins with a local stake and can remain meaningful without resolving the Glassed Hollow or the Remnant.

### 1. The case that never reached the court

A widow or tenant in a hot, damaged lower-ward building seeks an ordinary repair ruling. A petition alcove records ash and a case number, but the case never reaches the expected civic ledger. Yorven Khelt wants the repairs done; Ralenne wants a procedurally sound inquiry; a clerk fears the complaint exposes a concealed tunnel collapse. The resolution can secure housing or accountability. Later, a matching number in an archive can change the issue from bureaucratic failure to evidence that tunnel safety reports were being diverted. The cause may be incompetence, corruption, protective secrecy, or Remnant interference; it is not preassigned.

### 2. A school record and a frightened child

A family contests a school classification after a child freezes, flares, or refuses instruction following a household crisis. Theryn Dalewright sees a safety need; a Cinderwall neighbor sees class exclusion; a healer or Covenant member argues for rest and care. The local resolution is whether the child remains connected to school and family support. A later roll comparison may show a pattern of transfers, but it must distinguish trauma, relocation, ordinary administrative error, and covert recruitment rather than collapsing them into one conspiracy.

### 3. Guild work that resembles the war

Durnan Volkryst finds a contract specification that echoes a family memory of wartime forge labor. Selice Morra needs work to keep artisans employed; a merchant wants delivery; Kaevren questions why the order bypasses normal review. The immediate decision concerns a contract, a livelihood, and the safety threshold of a public craft. A later restricted archive or Volkhari testimony can reframe the specification as an attempt to revive amplification research.

### 4. The ward that answers its own bell

An altered shift-bell sequence appears to be the source of a worker's mistake, late meal, or missed pickup. Families in the affected ward reconstruct who heard what and why. The first outcome repairs a local schedule and recognizes a worker unfairly blamed for an accident. Much later, the sequence can match Deepbrand movement through maintenance routes. It does not prove that every bell, accident, or foreman is controlled from below.

### 5. The public rite that goes off script

Saelia Verithe prepares a civic rite intended to steady a frightened crowd. Seralynd worries that supplied revisions make grief too tidy; Serix has seen visions he did not create. The local stakes are whether the community can hold a public mourning without coercion or panic. A raw vision can point toward an archive, a survivor, or a physical clue, but cannot independently establish history. This makes faith, performance, and evidence different things.

### 6. Volkhari absence as a civic crime

A family rubbing from the Half-Name Wall conflicts with an official war account. A porter's ledger, a Thornfell cairn record, and a Mire name entry may later corroborate a missing person. The immediate outcome can be restoration of a name or recognition of an unknown family's loss. The larger reading leads toward erased Volkhari presence and the Hollow without claiming every missing name was Volkhari or every omission was made by the Remnant. Kaelith is free and should be approached as a person with choices, not a rescue object; Thalan remains the sole current captive.

### 7. The water that did not arrive

During a heat spell, a Cinderweave block's public wash and cooling allocation arrives short. Tenants accuse a nearby dye shop; the shop says it paid for its share; an Ashspire landlord claims the district steward rerouted the supply. The immediate stakes are hygiene, care for older residents, and whether workers lose a shift while they seek relief. Records and conversations can reveal an ordinary but consequential civic failure: an outdated occupancy count, an unreported new household, a damaged valve, or a steward protecting a favored building. The resolution corrects allocation and gives affected residents a way to challenge the tally. A later return may show that the block's hand-copied count became the basis for a fairer ward practice. This is deliberately not a Hollow clue, Remnant operation, magical anomaly, or hidden conspiracy.

## Relationships with the other regions

**Proposed relationship principles:** Emberholt needs food, routes, labor, witnesses, and legitimacy beyond its volcanic city. Its counterparts do not exist merely to condemn or supply it. Each relationship contains ordinary benefit, wartime grievance, and a present dispute over the meaning of restraint.

| Region | Everyday relationship | War and postwar inheritance | Present pressure and callback opportunity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Lowmark** | grain, river freight, seasonal migrants, and household remittances keep Emberholt kitchens and workshops functioning. | Emberholt demands and Lowmark supply records can be read as aid, coercion, or both depending on the convoy. | A freight tally and a missing Emberholt case number may identify civilians moved under a military-looking mark. Lowmark farm water spells make Emberholt's claim to be uniquely civilized by public magic politically hollow. |
| **Thornfell** | pass expertise, escort work, stone repair knowledge, and visiting ridge families connect practical mountain life. | A route closure, repair layer, or delayed signal can preserve incompatible accounts of who was abandoned. | The Seven Repairs bridge lets a Thornfell method reframe an Emberholt memorial story; it does not require a map claim until routes are verified. |
| **Mire** | convalescents, returnees, testimony, and trade pass through a region whose caretakers understand the cost of unnamed dead. | Emberholt's official account of war encounters Mire records that preserve uncertainty rather than heroic simplification. | A First Cooling verse or Half-Name entry can gain meaning through a Mire witness record. Emberholt visitors to the Mire can be workers returning to kin, not automatic agents of their government. |

No relationship assumes exact travel time, a contiguous four-region highway, or an ancient central administration. A cross-region editor must verify geography and select only connections supported by map work and local sources.
