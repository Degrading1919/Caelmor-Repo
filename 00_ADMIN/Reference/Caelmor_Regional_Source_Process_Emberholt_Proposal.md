# Caelmor Regional Source / Process Foundation — Emberholt Heights

**Status:** proposal-stage connective lore for Creative Director review. This brief establishes physical and social foundations from which later designers may derive material choices. It does not approve a resource, item, recipe, node, tier, schema, trade route, or production volume, and it does not amend live canon.

## Authority and limits

This proposal uses the reconciled Emberholt dossier, Timeline, War History, Factions, Parts 2–6, and the canon-reconciliation audit. Existing named special materials in legacy lists remain reference evidence of intended functions, not approved substances or production chains.

- Emberholt remains an industrial volcanic city whose ordinary civic heat and embercraft are legal under regional restraint.
- Weaponization, amplification, uncontrolled ritual experimentation, and dangerous research remain prohibited fault lines. Public craft does not excuse hidden research.
- Volkhari inherited fire attunement is distinct from learned human embercraft. This brief neither borrows Volkhari practice nor resolves C06.
- No source or process below depends on the precursor's identity or continuity under C03, a Founder link under C07, a speaking Stonebound, an exact route, or an exact map position.
- Lowmark remains the only full v1 region, and nothing here creates a player spellcasting or production system.

## Regional proposition

Emberholt's craft identity is stronger than its independent supply base. The city can concentrate heat, trained labor, inspection, and finishing work, but it cannot produce every conventional input from volcanic ground. Its material culture should therefore express a durable tension: Emberholt transforms much of what it uses, yet households and guilds remain dependent on carriers, growers, herders, cutters, miners, and witnesses beyond the city.

The Great War made that dependence coercive in places. The postwar settlement did not end industrial work; it made source records, safe limits, consent, cooling, waste handling, and the distinction between useful craft and dangerous research matters of public legitimacy.

## Foundation 1 — conventional ore and alloy inputs

| Required field | Proposed foundation |
|---|---|
| World source or import dependency | Volcanic and ridge workings can support ordinary stone extraction and some conventional mineral recovery, but the dossier does not justify a complete local ore base. Emberholt therefore combines locally recovered conventional feedstock, imported ore or worked stock, and carefully assessed ordinary scrap. No unique alloy or special ore is approved here. |
| Who gathers or imports it | Ridge crews, quarry and mine laborers, carriers, merchant houses, and receiving clerks form separate custody points. Outside supply may reach Emberholt through Lowmark markets or Thornfell-linked mountain exchange, without asserting an exclusive origin or direct road. |
| Who processes it | Ore dressers, furnace crews, forgeguild shops, repair smiths, and civic inspectors divide preparation, primary working, repair, and safety review. This keeps industrial authority from belonging to one office. |
| Broad processing method | Conventional material is sorted, broken, cleaned, heated or smelted where appropriate, worked, cooled, inspected, and reused when sound. Mixing inputs requires traceable workshop judgment; it does not create an approved alloy catalog. |
| Fuel and water needs | Vent heat and supervised embercraft can provide controlled heat or reheating, but they do not erase the need for conventional fuel, cooling water, washing, quenching, and safe heat removal. Civic water allocation is ordinary infrastructure, not water magic. |
| Waste and risk | Slag, scale, dust, fumes, sharp scrap, heat injury, polluted cooling water, unstable workings, and concealed inclusions are ordinary hazards. Resonant behavior or emotional instability triggers isolation and investigation rather than routine processing. |
| Stewardship and access | Civic safety offices, guild stewards, work crews, and district representatives have overlapping interests in workings, shifts, receiving records, cooling, and disposal. Exact constitutional powers remain open. Sealed war sites and the Glassed Hollow are outside ordinary access. |
| Scarcity reason | Scarcity follows safe mine access, variable imported supply, transport interruption, fuel and cooling limits, trained labor, postwar inspection, and the finite nature of sound scrap. It does not require a rare fantasy metal. |
| Regional cultural meaning | Emberholt judges respectable metalwork by dependable heat, visible workmanship, repairability, and whether a shop accepts responsibility for harm. Wartime prestige attached to output now competes with postwar pride in restraint. |
| Broad cross-region dependency | Thornfell can support mountain labor, stone and conventional metal precedent; Lowmark can mediate markets, freight, and food that keeps industrial labor operating. Exact deposits, cargos, and routes remain unselected. |

## Foundation 2 — fuel and industrial supply

| Required field | Proposed foundation |
|---|---|
| World source or import dependency | Emberholt has civic heat, vents, and fire-trained labor, but these are not a universal substitute for combustible fuel, furnace charge, kiln support, or clean cooling. The city depends on a mix of locally managed heat infrastructure and imported conventional fuel or fuel-making feedstock. |
| Who gathers or imports it | Vent crews maintain public heat access; carriers and merchant houses bring outside fuel; guild stores receive it; district workers move it to shops and communal facilities. No region is declared the exclusive supplier. |
| Who processes it | Fuel preparation and allocation belong to ordinary labor: drying, sizing, storing, tending, ash removal, and matching heat source to task. Embercraft instructors and safety staff supervise magical heat where it is used. |
| Broad processing method | Shops use direct civic heat where safe, conventional firing where the material process requires it, and staged cooling between work cycles. Heat is budgeted across homes, kitchens, baths, glass and ceramic work, and forges rather than treated as limitless. |
| Fuel and water needs | Industrial work consumes both heat and cooling capacity. Water must be stored, moved, and protected from contamination; a heat-rich city can still halt a shift when clean cooling is unavailable. |
| Waste and risk | Ash, smoke, fumes, hot residue, pressure changes, accidental ignition, exhausted workers, and damaged vent channels are visible costs. Dross districts must not become convenient sacrifice zones. |
| Stewardship and access | Vent keepers, district crews, guild stores, inspectors, and household representatives negotiate priority during shortage. Access to ordinary heat can be civic; access to high-intensity experimental arrangements is restricted. |
| Scarcity reason | Fuel scarcity reflects transport, drying time, safe storage, competition between household and industrial use, and postwar limits on extreme heat practice. Heat abundance does not mean safe process abundance. |
| Regional cultural meaning | A well-run works is expected to warm neighboring life rather than endanger it. Fuel records and cooling pauses carry moral weight because wartime schedules treated labor and heat as expendable. |
| Broad cross-region dependency | Emberholt's imported conventional fuel or fuel-making feedstock draws broadly on Lowmark and Thornfell managed-wood foundations without making either an exclusive supplier. Emberholt returns processed work and repair expertise without implying a fixed barter quota. |

## Foundation 3 — timber dependence

| Required field | Proposed foundation |
|---|---|
| World source or import dependency | The volcanic city does not have a canon-supported forest sufficient for construction, scaffolds, handles, carts, crates, mine support, or fuel. Most sound timber is imported; reclaimed postwar wood supplements it but is finite and must be inspected. |
| Who gathers or imports it | Outside woodland stewards and cutting crews supply carriers and merchants. Emberholt porters, receiving yards, builders, mine crews, and repair shops take custody within the city. |
| Who processes it | Yard workers sort and season stock; carpenters, builders, coopers or container makers in the broad sense, and repairers shape it for civic and industrial use. No named guild or product catalog is created. |
| Broad processing method | Timber is dried, graded by practical condition, cut, joined, repaired, and reclaimed where safe. Stock exposed to intense heat, fumes, war residue, or unstable resonance is not automatically reusable. |
| Fuel and water needs | Seasoning needs protected time and air; shaping needs modest workshop power and maintenance; fire protection and cooling water are essential around storage yards. Burning construction stock is a shortage decision, not a default. |
| Waste and risk | Fire, rot after cooling, hidden charring, splinters, dust, pests, unsafe mine supports, and fraudulent grading threaten workers. Offcuts can serve ordinary secondary uses only after safety needs are met. |
| Stewardship and access | Receiving yards and civic works reserve structurally sound stock for repairs and safe access. Guild purchasing power competes with lower-ward housing needs, creating an accountable political pressure without fixing constitutional authority. |
| Scarcity reason | Distance, seasonal cutting, route closures, seasoning time, urban fire risk, and competing regional construction make timber persistently valuable. |
| Regional cultural meaning | Reused beams can signify survival and repair, while visibly sound new timber signals wealth or public priority. Neither meaning requires a special species. |
| Broad cross-region dependency | Lowmark's managed river wood and Thornfell's bounded mountain timber are plausible broad sources after their own stewardship foundations are approved. The map does not establish a direct shipment path. |

## Foundation 4 — fibers, hides, and clothing work

| Required field | Proposed foundation |
|---|---|
| World source or import dependency | Emberholt's weavers and tannery workers depend mainly on imported ordinary fiber, cloth, hides, and tanning inputs. Local workshops can finish and adapt them for heat, soot, public color, work, ceremony, and status without implying a heat-proof fantasy fiber. |
| Who gathers or imports it | Growers, herders, hunters, household processors, carriers, and merchants outside the city supply raw or partly prepared inputs. Cinderweave receiving shops, household makers, dyers, tanners, and tailors complete or repair them. |
| Who processes it | Spinners and weavers where raw fiber arrives, dyers, tanners, cutters, stitchers, launders, and repairers share the chain. Not every stage needs to occur inside Emberholt. |
| Broad processing method | Ordinary cleaning, sorting, spinning, weaving or felting, curing, tanning, dyeing, cutting, stitching, controlled drying, and repeated repair provide the process range. Embercraft may provide supervised heat; it does not add unexplained magical properties. |
| Fuel and water needs | Washing, tanning, dyeing, rinsing, and worker cooling require clean water. Drying and dye baths require controlled heat. Competing demands make schedule and location socially important. |
| Waste and risk | Dirty water, tannery odor, hair and flesh waste, dye residue, fumes, burns, heat damage, and fire in dry stock create district burdens. Waste must be separated from household water and public cooling. |
| Stewardship and access | Workshop stewards and district authorities control shared vats, drains, heat windows, and fire separation. Workers and neighbors need a route to challenge harmful disposal; exact hearing authority remains open. |
| Scarcity reason | Crop or herd limits elsewhere, transport loss, clean-water demand, skilled finishing, and heat damage constrain supply. The finest display cloth can be scarce through labor and import dependence without a new substance. |
| Regional cultural meaning | Repaired work clothing marks competence and endurance; controlled color marks household, district, civic, mourning, or celebratory meaning. Public color can also expose class pressure and demands to display stability. |
| Broad cross-region dependency | Lowmark can ground plant fiber and hide supply; Thornfell can ground wool, hides, and weather-working knowledge. Mire relationships may support repair, care, and returned garments, but the Mire is not presumed a bulk textile source. |

## Foundation 5 — everyday food and import dependence

| Required field | Proposed foundation |
|---|---|
| World source or import dependency | Dense volcanic districts and industrial ground do not provide a complete food base. Emberholt relies on imported staples and preserved food, supplemented by whatever small household or sheltered cultivation later geography supports. This proposal does not select crops, herds, or recipes. |
| Who gathers or imports it | Producers and household processors in other regions supply carriers and merchant houses. Gate clerks, market workers, storekeepers, cooks, mutual-aid groups, and households receive and distribute it. |
| Who processes it | Bakers, cooks, preservers, public kitchens, guild kitchens, care workers, and households turn ordinary inputs into daily meals. No cuisine catalog is approved. |
| Broad processing method | Sorting, milling where appropriate, soaking, cooking, drying, smoking, fermenting, salting, and cool storage may be used as broad ordinary practices. Selection belongs to later food design. |
| Fuel and water needs | Heat is relatively available but still supervised; clean water and ingredients are the tighter limits. Kitchens compete with workshops for cooling water, safe vents, and labor during disruption. |
| Waste and risk | Spoilage, smoke, vermin, ash, grease, contaminated water, hoarding, and unequal rationing are practical risks. A complete gate record does not prove food reached every household. |
| Stewardship and access | Market inspectors, storekeepers, guild mutual aid, neighborhood kitchens, and household networks share responsibility. Wartime requisition memory makes priority claims politically sensitive. |
| Scarcity reason | Harvest failure elsewhere, closures, damaged crossings, merchant concentration, storage loss, and industrial purchasing power can all create shortage. Emberholt's heat cannot solve missing food. |
| Regional cultural meaning | Shared meals after a dangerous shift and food brought to a mourning or burned household turn imports into obligations, not merely commodities. Arguments over stores carry the memory of wartime requisition and postwar repair. |
| Broad cross-region dependency | Lowmark is the clearest broad staple and market partner. Thornfell may exchange preserved upland food, and Mire relationships may support herbs, care, and household exchange. No exact cargo or route is fixed. |

| Required field | Proposed foundation |
|---|---|
| World source or import dependency | Emberholt has established glassworking and intense controlled heat, but raw glass and ceramic inputs are not all proven local. Workshops therefore depend on selected ordinary mineral feedstock, clean cullet or shards, suitable clay where available through trade, and additives whose origins must be chosen later. Legacy named glass remains unapproved. |
| Who gathers or imports it | Quarry or sand workers, clay diggers, ash handlers, carriers, merchants, and receiving clerks supply glasswrights, potters, kiln crews, builders, and repair shops. These roles may span several regions. |
| Who processes it | Glasswrights and ceramic workers sort inputs, prepare batches or clay, form work, fire it, cool it, inspect it, and recover safe waste. Civic builders and households are users rather than proof of local source. |

| Fuel and water needs | Both practices require sustained controlled heat and careful cooling; clay and cleaning require water. Public heat makes production plausible but does not remove fuel accounting or worker exposure. |
| Waste and risk | Dust, fumes, sharp shards, kiln failure, thermal shock, contaminated glaze or color waste, water use, and discarded refractory matter burden nearby districts. Hollow or ritual-site glass is excluded from ordinary reuse. |

| Scarcity reason | Suitable clean input, transport, preparation, skilled cooling, breakage, and inspection constrain output. Heat alone cannot correct poor feedstock. |
| Regional cultural meaning | Transparent, colored, repaired, or deliberately dimmed surfaces can carry civic and mourning meaning because residents understand the labor and danger of controlled heat. They do not automatically store emotion or reveal history. |
| Broad cross-region dependency | Lowmark can plausibly supply river-borne ordinary sand or clay after local stewardship is established; Thornfell may supply mineral or stone-working knowledge; Mire clay remains possible only within its own access and firing limits. No source is exclusive. |

## Postwar industrial safety boundary

The postwar settlement supports three broad categories without inventing a statutory code:

1. **Ordinary lawful work:** household heat, repairs, standard smithing, textile finishing, tanning, food work, glass and ceramic work, and civic maintenance under practical safeguards.
2. **Heightened review:** unusually intense heat, unfamiliar input combinations, unstable workings, resonant behavior, sealed-site salvage, processes with district-scale effects, or work that cannot be safely cooled and witnessed.
3. **Prohibited wartime practice:** weaponization, magical amplification, coerced emotional loading, uncontrolled ritual experimentation, dangerous hidden research, and exploitation of Volkhari knowledge or bodies.

Records of source, custody, shift, accident, cooling, and disposal can support accountability, but they are not automatically truthful. Physical evidence, workers, households, civic offices, and outside suppliers retain different parts of the history.
