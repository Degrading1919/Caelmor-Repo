# Caelmor lore foundation — Creative Director review

**Status:** Proposal only. Nothing in this document changes canon. No equipment, resource catalog, mechanics, or spell system is designed here.

## Purpose and scope

Build a world that can be understood through ordinary work, contested records, inherited practices, geography, and later reinterpretation. All proposed stories remain grounded and solo-readable. Any anomaly should be rare, local, and ambiguous at first contact. The player is an ordinary participant who can uncover relationships between existing people and places, not a chosen savior.

This proposal draws on the Phase 1 vision and pillars, Master Assistant Protocol, Project Instructions, timeline, war history, factions, merged and detailed region dossiers, character anchors, and `World_&_Quest_Deep_Research.txt`. The detailed dossiers already contain regional quest anchors, settlements, threats, and crafting traditions; the additions below give them more civic history, human continuity, and cross-region consequences.

## Unresolved canon decisions

1. **The Mire's central site name and presentation.** Detailed dossier: Chapel of the Sinking Star; merged dossier: Sunken Chapel; character anchors: Sunken Church. Also specify whether “Chapel Shades” are perceptible earth-memory forms rather than literal ghosts, consistent with the detailed dossier.
2. **Volkhari population and terms.** Timeline/Factions describe a hidden enclave and fugitives while the merged overview says one survivor. Factions removes “High Smoke,” but character text retains it. Choose population scale and remove obsolete terminology.
3. **Postwar magic law.** War History's blanket outlawing cannot describe today's Emberholt and Director-approved Lowmark farming spells. Was it a brief failed continental edict, or a patchwork of regional restrictions from the start?
4. **Local government procedure.** Clarify how House Fenmere, village councils, and Lensemasters share authority, and how Thornfell's Assembly allocates seats and decides contested votes. The mechanisms below remain proposals.
5. **Emberholt office history.** Was the Speaker's office created after the war, or was an older office recast? Present-day stories can use the office without settling its origin.

These decisions should be reflected in source files with contradictory passages marked superseded, rather than hidden in new prose.

## Story structure to borrow, without borrowing content

Official RuneScape material demonstrates serial quest arcs, returns to familiar locations, standalone local stories, and small secrets attached to place and activity. See [RuneScape game information](https://www.runescape.com/game-info), [Old School Dragon Slayer II poll](https://oldschool.runescape.com/polls/2017/1462), [Myreque finale poll](https://oldschool.runescape.com/polls/2025/1700), and [Havenhythe](https://rs.runescape.com/havenhythe). The usable structural lessons for Caelmor are:

1. Give a local story its own human resolution while allowing one detail to recur later.
2. Let terrain, routine speech, and records corroborate or dispute each other.
3. Revisit an early landmark with new context rather than adding a new exposition site.
4. Keep an explicit ladder for each mystery: rumor, physical sign, conflicting account, corroboration, then a later reversal or bounded uncertainty.
5. Reward recognition with understanding and social consequences. Do not require every optional clue to follow the main story.

## First-pass gap audit

The existing canon has strong thematic regional identities, major quest anchors, and distinctive landscapes. It is thinner in the long interval before magical rediscovery; the civic systems that kept communities alive after the war; the practical sequence of wartime evacuation, supply, and missing-person records; ordinary relationships between regions; and repeatable social traditions whose meaning changes when a player learns their history. Existing mysteries lean heavily on supernatural sites. They need more mundane records, inherited mistakes, and people with different incentives to tell the truth. Emberholt needs more ordinary citizens who sincerely benefit from civic life, while the Mire needs life beyond mourning. Thornfell's politics need stakes beyond a two-bloc war/peace argument. Lowmark's gentleness needs visible costs and procedures.

## Proposed Lowmark expansion

**Civic life.** The *Seven Reaches Compact* is an annual thaw agreement among river wards and settlements. It allocates flood labor, ferry closures, channel clearing, and emergency stores. House Fenmere regards it as custom; small villages regard it as their only reliable check on noble control of mills and crossings. The *Ledger Court* at Crestbridge hears harvest-season toll, inheritance, and cargo disputes with a Fenmere keeper, a Lensemaster observer, and a rotating village witness. Records are useful but imperfect: people unable to afford travel or witnesses may disappear from the official story. The *Ferrymen's Measure* permits a crossing refusal for weather, structural danger, or a named memorial day. Breaking that custom gives Maelin's route choices a social cost. Orchard Circles rotate seasonal labor and child care; their wordless work songs retain a missing war verse that older households dispute.

**Places with three readings.** *Bramblelock Weir* is an overgrown wartime sluice. To orchard owners it is a nuisance; to an engineer its numbered stones show an old controlled route; to a careful reader they match a convoy omission in Fenmere records. *The Sluice Chapel* is a roofless working flood shelter, not a new religious order. Child-height repair marks and names from several regions show continuous use by refugees and laborers. *Widow's Step* is an old embankment where families leave a small unmatched household scrap for people lost to the water. Floods carry some scraps toward Mire memorial routes. At *Reed-Lantern Bend*, screened lamps and practical patrol rules make a night crossing safe; a later Mire visit makes the lamp arrangement newly recognizable. An older mill at *Drowsebank* has repairs that contradict a family's accepted account of how grain moved in the war. Keep its apparent oddness physically plausible unless Lowmark's magical limits are settled.

**Stories that outlive their first quest.** In *The Empty Tithe*, Corvin finds that Ashenstead's relief exemption is being bought by prosperous landholders. The resolution corrects a present tax injustice while showing how an old mercy became marketable privilege. In *Joric's Furrow*, a buried guide line leads to a former civilian treatment site and names omitted from a memorial. Families disagree about public marking, private notification, and formal correction. *A Barge Arrives Lighter* begins with a sealed letter in ordinary cargo. Its wrapping bears a Mire stitch, its addressee appears on a Thornfell name shelf, and an Emberholt clerk values it for reasons that remain uncertain. Tovin's repeated sketches of water and mill floors can later help a bridgewright identify a repair sequence; Tovin never needs to become a mage or historian. A junior Lensemaster wants prewar river maps made public to correct noble claims; an elder fears renewed looting of dangerous routes. Both positions deserve practical evidence.

**Material customs before materials.** A bargeman ties a blue thread inside one cuff on the first thaw run and removes it on returning. An old unremoved thread first reads as grief; later it identifies a missing passage. Bridge repairers stamp a stone's hidden face with season, crew, and flood height, creating a practical record that Thornfell workers can read. A guest receives the first poured cup of water in a Lowmark house; refusing it has meaning in negotiation. Memorial gardens put ground cover, reeds, and fruiting growth in a usual order; households subtly change the order for the named, unreturned, and those deliberately omitted. These are social practices, not item specifications.

## Proposed Thornfell expansion

**Institutions and divided interests.** At the *Open Rope*, a Bridgewright, a senior traveler, and an apprentice publicly inspect a crossing before the thaw. A closure redirects grain, patrols, and Assembly attendance, giving maintenance political force. *Winter Share* requires each Highstead to witness its shelter and food tally before the first heavy snow. Blood-Oath speakers want protected stores; Hearthguard speakers fear private hoarding. The *Unclaimed Names Shelf* holds people with disputed clan, origin, or allegiance. Three independent witnesses are needed before Maera assigns a cairn stone, making refugees and war omissions a live civic question. At Passwatch, a Warden, a Bridgewright, and a civilian quartermaster each hold a key to opening a closed route. The three-key procedure grew from a remembered misuse of emergency power. In Stonewake Hall, traditional listening benches have uneven acoustics. Hadrik's seating is political even if no supernatural cause is accepted.

**Places.** *Nettlehook Highstead* is a mixed-clan shelf community dependent on a ropeway; an apparently stolen bucket has carried unregistered family messages. *Hushfold* began as an exile camp and has become a permanent hamlet of clanless families, widows, and children. Every bloc needs its labor, yet no bloc agrees on its Assembly voice. *Black Glass Hostel*, once a siege infirmary, bars clan colors and keeps annual snow-height marks. One erased mark belongs to an Emberholt medic, reminding visitors that enemy lines did not describe every individual act. *The Far Bell Cut* warns of rockfall, but the bell is unheard from one neighboring hollow; a rescue story explains the local safety rule before any later investigation considers resonance. *The Red Slope Terraces* retain damaged defensive walls. Younger families want to reclaim the ground, while veterans' descendants fear disturbing a place where a retreat came too late.

**Stories.** *The Third Horn* concerns an omitted reply in an old retreat code. Dorn removed it from instruction after a false alarm; Iven later hears a similar interval at the empty pass. The eventual testimony concerns people stranded outside a gate, not a ghost army. *The Weight of a Name* asks whether a Hushfold family can claim a disputed cairn; Lowmark shipping and Mire stitching complicate an oath-stone account. *The Bridge that Chose* forces a safe crossing plan when sentries, grain carriers, and families all need passage. Bridgewright testimony prevents a clean heroic answer; later NPC routines reveal which need was deferred. In *Ash in the Snow*, genuine Emberholt papers and possible forgeries travel together. Brynjar's alarm, Elira's caution, and Ressa's responsibility to prevent retaliation are all understandable. *Cairns Below the Waterline* links local name-stones to Lowmark repair marks and Mire memorial work, exposing a civilian network overlooked by military accounts.

**Material customs before materials.** A bridge's woven load braid incorporates contributions from the settlements that depend on it. Replacements after major repairs preserve a visible history of migration and mutual labor. Flat temporary name-slips remain in a Cairn-Keeper's roll until testimony allows permanent placement. House shutters carry family repair marks; marks from several clans indicate an old refuge. Cups pass in one direction at ordinary meals and the reverse at mourning meals, allowing a quiet early visual clue to become legible later.

## Proposed Mire expansion

**Governance and work.** The *Names Below Compact* records recovered dead without first sorting them by wartime allegiance. Each entry states the place, a witness, and what remains unknown. This explains why banner workers may resist a visitor's demand for a heroic identity. A monthly open-page reading makes unresolved cases public. The *Reedsward* is a household obligation to maintain paths, ferry moorings, lantern stakes, and flood warnings; it is civic labor, not a militia. Rotating crews give Edris practical authority and make weather, work, and gossip part of everyday life. *Silt Courts*, composed of Chapel-Tenders, Memorykeepers, and household witnesses, mediate grave disturbance, route claims, recovered property, and old debt. Their rulings depend on cooperation, which makes witness pressure and missing pages serious threats. The *Lantern Debt* records acts of rescue and upkeep owed between households and visitors; payment does not simply erase the obligation.

**Places.** At *The Dry Muster*, a gap in drill lines first looks like a collapse of discipline; a later letter says people broke ranks to carry wounded civilians. *The Nine Stakes* records nine conflicting attempts to mark a safe crossing; the ninth protected a fleeing group by diverting pursuit into difficult ground. *Rookery House* has flood-height marks interrupted during war years, then resumed in another hand. *The Unfired Kiln* at Rootrest became a children's rain shelter after its makers left for an emergency retreat; a list of promised memorial markers survives in the wall. *Saint Orra's Step* is a causeway where funeral bearers briefly lower their burden. A merchant's refusal to pause begins a dispute about visitor obligations, not a fight. *The Hushed Granary* is a jointly maintained emergency store with keys held by three settlements, so one missing key becomes a civic mystery.

**Stories beyond anomalies.** *A Banner With No Side* starts with a Thornfell-stitched cloth repaired in a Lowmark style. The likely history is a field treatment crew serving refugees irrespective of army, yet no record needs to give one final account. *The Borrowed Winter* finds Lowmark grain and Thornfell labor in the same survival ledger; present people disagree about whether relief creates a debt. *The Empty Place* begins when a child leaves a work token overnight at the family mourning table. Adults interpret it as a request to search for an absent relative, which leads to records, ferry schedules, and a living person who moved away. *The Hushed Granary Key* asks whether a missing key signals theft or a neighbor bypassing slow rules during a flood. A dispute over causeway maintenance can put Marshfall and Sorrowmere at odds without an enemy or supernatural event. A returning worker from Emberholt can be welcomed as kin and distrusted as a bearer of the city's official war story at the same meal.

**Material customs before materials.** A second, unmarked place at a bereavement meal makes space for unreturned people and for names unsafe to speak. Guests leave a sign of their daily work there and take it home; an abandoned sign asks for action. Banner retirement is a documented labor of washing, mending, witness-taking, and either storage or respectful letting-go, not a prompt to identify magical cloth. Raised paths show many hands and repair methods. Different ages can read the same joinery as flood history, a neighbor's favor, or a family quarrel.

## Proposed Emberholt expansion

**Civic life with a hidden cost.** The *Hearth Tally* is a yearly household account of training, flame accidents, and safety needs. Most citizens cooperate because it genuinely directs instruction and emergency care. A concealed use of the same record for selecting useful subjects is a later discovery; the early story is a family correcting a mistaken tally. *Cooling Houses* offer communal rest after accidents, grief, or difficult shifts. Sincere volunteers and doctrinal monitors can occupy the same room. *The Measure of Ash* is an inquest practice for major fire accidents: what was preventable, what was hidden, and who carries the cost. Ashfall-aligned advisers gradually crowd out neutral witnesses. District shift bells and shared meals create a social clock. A changed bell sequence that first appears to cause one worker's mistake later maps to covert tunnel movements. The lower ward's *Heat Commons* coordinates shared washing, warmth, child care, and repair access, giving Cinderwall residents civic agency and leverage.

**Places.** At *The Cold Seam*, children leave written apologies in an unusually cool wall fissure. Its practical vent function is discoverable; repeated phrasing in some notes later suggests guided dreams or instruction. *The Bridge of Seven Repairs* is a visible history of successive civic priorities, not a magical structure. Thornfell workers recognize one repair as a wartime method. *The Quiet Annex* holds pupils whose fire “does not answer”; a kind teacher's records show many are frightened or exhausted, then some vanish from normal rolls. *The Ashwater Cistern* serves emergencies, while its guarded maintenance routes tie public safety to controlled access. *The Half-Name Wall* has surnames removed from a war memorial. Family rubbings, a Thornfell cairn, and a Mire entry can jointly restore one identity. *The Red Door Clinic* treats burns without asking for civic status; testimony there later changes the reading of an “individual carelessness” report.

**Stories and politics.** *The Missing Petition* begins with a widow whose ordinary infrastructure complaint never reaches a civic ledger. A matching case number elsewhere turns the disappearance into evidence of a concealed tunnel collapse. *Seven Repairs* lets a bridgeworker's family believe a patch honors one hero until other regions reveal a collective rescue. *The Unapproved Verse* joins a removed Emberholt remembrance lyric to a Mire mourning form, without asserting active magic. Forge families can disagree between continuity of employment and public witness to dangerous contracts. Emberguard field officers may prioritize evacuation while administrators optimize containment reports. Covenant circles can disagree over whether disciplined grief requires quiet or naming harm in public. Trade officials can impose incident-related safety surcharges, which merchants and households in all regions feel immediately.

**Material customs before materials.** *The First Cooling* lowers household flames at the war's annual remembrance and displays repaired work. The Speaker presents recovery; families privately remember omissions. Petition alcoves retain an ash and case-number trail even when a request is never copied. Visible repair layers on bridges, vents, and homes record which district received care and which waited. Work bells and shared meals mark neighborhood belonging more reliably than grand ceremony.

## Proposed civilian history spine

All dates below are approximate and subject to a timeline rewrite. They describe what ordinary people built and inherited; they do not create a new magical origin, divine revelation, or military victory account. The Director has placed the 5–10 year war at roughly 40–30 years before the present and Dreamers after it.

**The long withdrawal, about 3,000–500 years before the present.** When direct divine communion faded, households could no longer rely on an expected answer from above. Lowmark river settlements kept flood and crossing notes in repeated knot patterns and marks at landings. Thornfell ledge households gave guest-right to stranded travelers in return for route work, the ancestor of bridge and winter arbitration. Mire communities raised name posts on safe ground, each post assigning someone the mundane task of renewing it after flood. Vent-side Emberholt districts counted dwellings during smoke and tremors so every household was checked. These practices arose from weather and mutual dependence. They should never be presented as forgotten spell systems.

**The centuries of local route-making, then the rediscovery era beginning about 250 years before the present.** River landings, ridge refuges, marsh channels, and upland paths formed a four-part civilian circuit. Each segment needed local knowledge; no pan-Caelmor ministry controlled it. As listening traditions re-emerged, the same route culture acquired new interpretations, but its civic purpose remained. Lowmark copyholders separated weather loss, household loss, and trade debt. Thornfell standardized storm, injury, and blockage calls. Mire witness tables recognized kin and property through ferrymen, midwives, and chapel hosts. Emberholt district heat wards coordinated inspection and instruction. The distinction between first rediscoverers and later Founders must be observed in any eventual ancient-person narrative. Dreamers do **not** belong in this prewar period.

**The 5–10 war years, ending about 30 years before the present.** In the first disruptions, military escorts displaced ordinary carriers and route hosts began duplicating load lists at each transfer. During strain, Lowmark mills and barges fed civilians as well as armies; Thornfell refuges became way stations; Mire guides marked safer channels; Emberholt ward workers kept damaged districts habitable alongside directed production. As routes fragmented, households distrusted unsealed orders and hid spare capacity. The most durable testimony became mundane: departure and arrival tallies, bridge crews, ferry schedules, letters, and unclaimed names. The war's end was felt differently at the last convoy, horn reply, burial, and district roll call. Different local cessation dates can be culturally true while the global span remains 5–10 years. The Glassed Hollow truth follows the Director's decisions but is outside this civilian account.

**Years 1–3 after cessation: the Counting.** Settlement leaders counted survivors, identified unsafe routes, reunited separated households, and copied names before temporary markers vanished. Lowmark put inspections at river landings; Thornfell reopened passes one at a time; Mire councils published channel boards; Emberholt neighborhoods resumed heat and water checks. Public record-keeping gained power because it determined who received aid and which places were considered inhabited.

**Years 4–8: repair compacts.** Mixed crews crossed old lines under local protection. Lowmark advanced food against future labor; Thornfell sent bridge and retaining-wall teams; Mire guides reopened dependable channels; Emberholt guild crews repaired damaged civic works. Every region can honestly claim it paid a larger share of the cost. This is the proposed origin of many visible repair marks and of an evacuation survey later mistaken for boundary stones.

**Years 9–14: memorial disputes.** Communities had to decide whether to leave scars, reclaim land, or conceal shame. Lowmark allowed some burned fields to become new boundaries. Thornfell argued about the order of names on cairns. Mire households disagreed over when salvage became grave disturbance. Emberholt placed prominent damage inside a disciplined recovery narrative while lower wards kept their own accounts. This period also made survivor testimony politically valuable while witnesses were still alive, consistent with a war 30 years ago.

**Years 15–20: a second generation of records.** Children who knew rationing rather than battle entered work and public service. A wayhouse list became a toll precedent; a relief note became an inheritance claim; a heat-safety roll became a classification. The proposed *Four Lamps* observance arose when all four civilian route segments were again dependable. Lowmark kept a river light, Thornfell a pass beacon, the Mire a covered lantern, and Emberholt a lowered household flame. Each region now explains the same custom differently. Dreamers can begin appearing sometime after the war, but their precise date and relation to these civic changes require a separate approved account.

**The last decade to the present.** Records and customs are old enough to feel inherited and recent enough that living adults contest them. Former child evacuees now run workshops, courts, ferries, and watch rotations. The Remnant's wartime precursor has had time to reorganize, conceal evidence, and influence public policy; the formal Remnant's exact founding year should be set in the revised timeline. The player enters a landscape where testimony, physical repairs, and official files disagree in specific, testable ways.

### Four proposed practical links between regions

1. **Fourfold Measure:** cargo weights and load limits descend from emergency coordination. Lowmark clerks, Thornfell bridgeworkers, Mire cooperative weighers, and Emberholt inspectors interpret an inherited conversion differently. It creates trade friction and a chain of records, not a central government.
2. **Wayhouse Accord:** independent shelters grant a dry bed to rescuers, displaced people, and carriers of sealed family notices, repaid through labor. Maelin, Lessa, a Passwatch host, and Rowan can pass news through this chain at plausible speed, each with a partial account.
3. **Names Exchange:** once a year, Lowmark memorial keepers, Thornfell Cairn-Keepers, Mire Chapel-Tenders, and a participating Emberholt civic clerk compare copied lists of missing people. Annelise, Maera, Calwen, and the Emberholt clerk each possess only one kind of evidence.
4. **Fair Weather Line:** route observers relay flood, landslip, fog, and ash notices by lamps, boards, barges, and safe horn codes. Tovin, Iven, a Mire lantern-bearer, and an Emberholt surveyor see different parts of the same disruption. Errors can be innocent or deliberately introduced.

These are proposed networks of obligations and records, not new factions or game systems.

### Proposed Emberholt diplomatic process

The *Four-Seal Passage Process* is a public Civic Council procedure for trade, travel, and border grievances, not a new sovereign body. A visitor files a numbered petition at Embergate and receives a response date. A route, toll, or compensation agreement requires recorded views from Trade Commissioner Oren Falx, Magister Vael Trossen, a Border-Warden, and a rotating district overseer before the Speaker countersigns. Neighboring regions provide witnesses through their existing offices rather than newly invented embassies. An urgent passage warrant expires after thirty days without public review. This process gives the Speaker a credible public government, other regions a way to object, and Ashfall-aligned actors a concrete procedure to manipulate. The Director should approve the exact legal powers before it becomes canon.

One proposed umbrella mystery, the *Quiet Survey*, can hold the repair and route clues together. It should reveal a civilian safety network in stages, rather than a single conspiracy document. Its first payoff is solidarity: ordinary people built passages across political lines. A later, separate question asks who altered or buried specific records. Do not reflexively assign every omission to the Remnant; theft, shame, protective secrecy, and clerical error should all occur where evidence supports them.

### Six layered places for revisits

1. **Reed-Knot Landing, Lowmark:** a children's knot game first seems to echo old flood marks; a freight dispute exposes duplicate cargo lines; only a later Mire name links the marks to evacuation destinations.
2. **Three Calls Shelter, Thornfell:** guest-right rules and patched beams explain its use; children's height marks behind a route map show it sheltered families who departed in different directions and generated incompatible later accounts.
3. **Silt Ledger Steps, Mire:** a chapel approach holds copied temporary names and water-depth marks; a missing relief-list name reappears in a Lowmark copy, changing an apparent burial error into a possible deliberate alteration.
4. **Ashen Ward Court, Emberholt:** public heat notices cover an older refuge yard; wall niches and inspection marks reveal which households were checked during a smoke event and whose names were left out.
5. **The Broken Span, Lowmark–Thornfell approach:** toll complaints are ordinary; repair signatures from four regions and an old depot orientation later show that a noble route claim rests on civilian convoy work.
6. **The Four Lamps Mile, Mire–Emberholt road:** four household-maintained lights first signal safe passage; dialect and repair dates reveal four route systems met there; one lamp stayed shuttered after a recovery-year disagreement.

## Older layers and restrained mysteries

The civic spine should not make the world's antiquity begin with the Great War. The following proposed observations add depth without resolving the Forgotten God's silence or turning every ruin into a device.

1. **Pre-magic work persists.** Lowmark flood marks, Thornfell guest ledges, Mire name posts, and Emberholt evacuation tallies begin in the long withdrawal. Later Riverwardens, Echohewn workers, Memorywardens, and embercraft institutions inherited some spaces and changed their meanings. A site can therefore contain a mundane older layer, a rediscovery-era interpretation, a wartime use, and a present repair.
2. **Echohewn construction has multiple functions.** A Thornfell sound channel may carry warnings efficiently by ordinary acoustics, while one rare place holds an unexplained dormant response. Most buildings should be useful architecture. Iven, Dorn, Hadrik, and Kaldren can disagree about them without the story proving that every echo is magic.
3. **The Five Vent Chapels are proposed Emberholt shelters.** Before the present doctrine, ridge households kept food, water, names, and evacuation rules at unstable vents. Some later civic rites reuse those forms; lower ward maintenance uses old passages. One chapel can show evidence of historic Volkhari–human coexistence, another a war refuge, another postwar record loss. They are social places above older Stonebound-adjacent works, not five new dungeons or active spell sites.
4. **Mire ground holds testimony, not automatic truth.** A trench hum can have a drainage explanation, a Chapel-Tender's emotional reading, and a buried warning cadence. Memory effects remain rare and place-bound. The story should never use them as perfect recordings that overrule people.
5. **Lowmark's practical magic needs social limits.** Under the Director's decision, ordinary farming workers know simple water spells. A proposed village custom is that such work is taught beside irrigation and flood safety, witnessed by older hands, and discussed as husbandry rather than mastery. The Last Field-Mage's combat knowledge is a different, exceptional inheritance. The distinction creates tension when an Emberholt visitor calls both practices “training,” without inventing spell lists or gameplay systems.
6. **Volkhari culture should have a civilian face.** Beyond warnings, captivity, and prophecy, propose domestic rites of cooling after anger, shared recitation of places lost, disputes over whether a hidden community owes aid to endangered outsiders, and patient maintenance of volcanic paths. Their exact population and biographies remain pending. These practices give them motives other than suffering or serving as a source of power.
7. **Dreams are postwar experiences.** The Dreamers may reopen questions about the world before withdrawal, but no proposal here proves divine command, time travel, or a single cause. Early Dreamer testimony should be fragmentary, contradicted by practical evidence where appropriate, and difficult to use as political authority.

### Proposals rejected or constrained during reconciliation

- No broad resurrection, literal ghost population, or Mire necromancy. Rare major threats and minor anomalies follow the Director's limit.
- No assumption that all Thornfell acoustics are magical, nor that every Echohewn building is a hidden weapon. Most remain ordinary useful construction.
- No claim that all four regions shared one ancient unified empire or formal central administration. Their route culture can connect them without flattening regional identities.
- No clean moral division between regions. Emberholt has sincere care, useful civic training, and internal dissent; other regions have class exclusion, hoarding, forged records, and political evasions.
- No Remnant authorship assigned to every missing name or suspicious document. That would shrink the world and erase local human motives.
- No new player casting, equipment, resource tiers, loot, or progression gates. Lore observations remain lore observations until a separately approved design pass.
- No “chosen one” Dreamer interpretation for the player. Dreams and environmental memory remain uncertain sources of knowledge.

## Material-culture readiness, without designing materials

**Lowmark:** flood management, grain and ferry work, glass and household care, first-cup hospitality, seasonal repair marks, and memorial planting now have social purposes and historical layers. **Thornfell:** bridge inspection, load braids, guest-right, name-slips, winter stores, shutter marks, and horn education arise from terrain and political obligations. **Mire:** path maintenance, ferries, name recording, banner retirement, shared meals, marker setting, and care houses arise from flood, migration, and grief. **Emberholt:** heat safety, public petitions, worker meals, cooling spaces, inquests, school records, and visible repairs arise from civic utility and hidden control.

Across regions, proposed traditions of repair marks, carrying cloth, mourning meals, and hazard thresholds have related functions but distinct forms. That gives a future design pass a way to derive objects and material choices from lived needs, climate, labor, and memory. **No object, gear, ingredient, node, or resource category is approved or specified by this document.**

Four proposed lineages make that derivation traceable. **Repair marks:** Lowmark bridge undersides track flood and crew; Thornfell shutters and spans track storm repairs; Mire path posts track sinking and rerouting; Emberholt retaining walls track heat damage and council funding. **Carrying cloth:** Lowmark families patch rain covers for grain travel, Thornfell households incorporate steep-route binding patterns, Mire boat crews use recognizable knots, and Emberholt workers mark ash-cleaning duties. A patch from another region can indicate marriage, migration, relief work, or resale; context decides which. **Mourning meals:** Lowmark's first cup, Thornfell's reversed passing order, the Mire's second place, and Emberholt's lowered flame offer distinct ways to feed and acknowledge survivors after communal work. **Threshold signs:** flood notches, rope loops, raised reed bundles, and ash-wash lines tell a local whether a dwelling is safe to enter after the region's common hazard. Their shared purpose supports recognition without making the cultures visually interchangeable.
